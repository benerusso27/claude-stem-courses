## fig2_shearflow (.svg / .png)
**Figure 2. Horizontal shear and the shearing-stress distribution**

Three panels. (a) A short length delta x of a beam, cut horizontally above the neutral axis. The normal stresses on the part above the cut are larger on the right face, where the moment is M plus delta M, than on the left, so a horizontal force delta H equal to q times delta x must act on the cut; the shaded area above the cut is A prime with its centroid at y bar prime. (b) In a rectangle the shearing stress varies as a parabola through the depth, zero at the top and bottom and largest, 3V over 2A, at the neutral axis. (c) In an I-section the stress is small in the flanges, jumps up where the width drops to the web thickness, and is nearly uniform across the web.

## fig3_measure (.svg / .png)
**Figure 3. Measuring shear strain, and shearing stress across the width**

Two panels. (a) A square element in pure shear distorts into a rhombus; one diagonal lengthens and the other shortens by half the shear strain, so a pair of gages at plus and minus 45 degrees measures the shear strain as the difference of their readings, and the axial strain from bending cancels. (b) Shearing stress on the neutral axis divided by the elementary value VQ over Ib, plotted across the width of rectangles with b over h of 0.25, 0.5, 1 and 2 for Poisson's ratio 0.3: narrow sections are nearly uniform, but as the section gets wider the stress rises at the faces and falls at the center while its average stays equal to the elementary value.

## fig4_timoshenko (.svg / .png)
**Figure 4. Shear deformation**

Two panels. (a) A beam segment deforms in two ways: in bending its cross-sections rotate but stay normal to the axis; in shear they slide past each other, so the axis tilts by the shear strain gamma equal to f s V over GA. The deflection is the sum of the two. (b) Log-log plot of the total deflection divided by the bending-only deflection against span-to-depth ratio for a simply supported beam with a central load, for a rectangle and for a typical I-section, Poisson's ratio 0.3. Both curves approach 1 for long spans and rise steeply for short ones; the I-section's curve lies well above the rectangle's, so it needs a longer span before shear falls below 5 percent of the deflection, marked by a horizontal line.

## fig5_layered (.svg / .png)
**Figure 5. Built-up beams: loose, partially connected and bonded**

Three panels for a beam made of two equal layers with a central load. (a) Strain through the depth at midspan: loose layers bend separately, each with its own neutral axis, so the strain jumps at the interface; a bonded beam has one straight strain line through the whole depth; a partially connected beam lies in between, with a smaller jump. (b) Slip between the layers along the span, divided by the loose end slip: largest at the supports and zero at midspan, and much smaller and confined near the supports as the connection parameter alpha L grows. (c) Effective bending stiffness divided by the loose value against alpha L on a log scale: it rises from 1 for a loose stack to 4 for a fully bonded one, with most of the gain between alpha L of about 1 and 30.
