## fig1_setup (.svg / .png)
**Figure 1. Tension-test setup**

Schematic of a universal testing machine. A moving crosshead at the top, carrying a load cell, travels upward along two screw columns at a set rate while the base stays fixed. Wedge grips hold the threaded or shouldered ends of a round specimen. A clip-on extensometer with a 50.0 mm gage length is attached to the narrow reduced section, whose initial diameter is 10.00 mm. An arrow labeled P shows the tensile load measured by the load cell.

## fig2_specimen_fbd (.svg / .png)
**Figure 2. Specimen geometry and free-body diagram**

Part a: side view of a round tension specimen with wider grip ends, fillets, and a reduced section 60 mm long and 10.00 mm in diameter. Two gage marks 50.0 mm apart are centered on the reduced section. Shaded zones about one diameter long next to each fillet are labeled as regions of non-uniform stress. Part b: free-body diagram of the lower piece of the gage section below a cut a–a. The grip pulls down with force P; on the cut face a uniform tensile stress sigma acts upward over the original area A0. Equilibrium of vertical forces gives sigma times A0 minus P equals zero, so sigma equals P over A0.

## fig3_stress_strain (.svg / .png)
**Figure 3. Anatomy of the stress-strain diagram**

Two plots of an illustrative material, not the lab specimens. Part a, engineering stress in MPa versus engineering strain in percent from 0 to 34 percent for a mild steel: a steep linear elastic line up to an upper yield point near 360 MPa, a drop to a flat lower-yield Luders plateau near 340 MPa extending to about 2 percent strain, a rising strain-hardening curve to a maximum of about 500 MPa near 18 percent strain, then a shaded necking region where engineering stress falls to fracture at 32 percent. A dash-dot true-stress curve, plotted up to the maximum load only, lies above the engineering curve and keeps rising. Part b, aluminum-like curve with gradual yielding over 0 to 1.2 percent strain: a dashed line of slope E starting at 0.2 percent strain cuts the curve at the 0.2 percent offset yield strength, marked by a diamond near 250 MPa.

## fig4_compliance (.svg / .png)
**Figure 4. Why crosshead travel overstates strain**

Left: schematic of two springs in series between a fixed base and the moving crosshead. The lower spring represents the frame, grips and specimen shoulders with compliance C m; the upper spring represents the specimen reduced section with compliance L c over E A0. Both carry the same load P, and the crosshead travel Delta is the sum of their stretches. Right: engineering stress up to 280 MPa versus strain up to 0.6 percent for an illustrative steel. The extensometer curve rises steeply with slope E; the crosshead-based curve starts with a curved toe from grip seating and then rises with a much smaller slope E app, given by 1 over E app equals 1 over E plus A0 C m over L c.

## fig5_considere (.svg / .png)
**Figure 5. Considère construction for the onset of necking**

Two stacked plots sharing a true-strain axis from 0 to 0.45, for an illustrative power-law material with n = 0.22. Top: load per original area rises, peaks at true strain 0.22, then falls. Bottom: the true-stress curve rises with decreasing slope while the dotted hardening-rate curve falls steeply from high values; they cross at true strain 0.22, marked with a circle and labeled Considère: d sigma t over d epsilon t equals sigma t, so the necking strain equals n. A dashed vertical line links the crossing to the load maximum. To the left the region is labeled stable uniform stretching; to the right a shaded region is labeled unstable, neck grows.
