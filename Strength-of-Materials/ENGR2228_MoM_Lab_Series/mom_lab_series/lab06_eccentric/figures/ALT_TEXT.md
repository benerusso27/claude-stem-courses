## fig2_kern (.svg / .png)
**Figure 2. Eccentric load in a plane of symmetry, and the kern**

Two panels. (a) Stress across the depth of a rectangular section loaded at a quarter of the depth above the centroid: a uniform block P over A plus a linear bending distribution, minus M z y over I z, gives a combined distribution that is tension over most of the depth and compression near the bottom; the neutral axis, where the combined stress is zero, lies below the centroid, on the side away from the load. (b) A rectangle with its kern, a diamond whose half-diagonals are h over 6 along y and b over 6 along z. Three load points on the y axis are shown with their neutral axes: a load inside the kern puts the neutral axis outside the section, a load on the kern boundary puts it exactly on the bottom edge, and a load outside the kern brings it into the section. The farther the load moves out, the closer the neutral axis comes to the centroid.

## fig3_principal (.svg / .png)
**Figure 3. Unsymmetric section: principal axes and the neutral axis**

Two panels. (a) A generic unequal-leg angle, 60 by 40 by 5 millimeters, drawn with y up and z to the left, loaded in tension at the outside corner of the tip of its long leg. The section is shaded by normal stress, orange for tension and blue for compression. Dash-dot lines mark the principal axes u (major) and v (minor), which are tilted from the legs. A solid line marks the true neutral axis, which crosses the short leg; a purple dash-dot line, labelled wrong, shows where the neutral axis would be if the product of inertia were ignored. A grey quadrilateral near the centroid is the kern. (b) The second moment of area about an axis at angle theta from z, and the product of inertia in the rotated axes, plotted against theta from minus 90 to 90 degrees: the second moment is largest exactly where the product of inertia is zero, which defines the principal angle theta p.

## fig4_beamcolumn (.svg / .png)
**Figure 4. When the deformation changes the eccentricity**

Two panels. (a) A bar between two rigid end blocks that are pinned on the load line, a distance a beyond each end of the free length L; the bar's centerline starts a distance e from the load line. Under tension the bar bends toward the load line and the eccentricity at mid-length shrinks; under compression the bar bows away from the load line and the eccentricity grows. (b) The bending moment divided by P times e, plotted against the axial load as a fraction of the buckling load of the member with its end blocks. At mid-length the curves for end blocks of zero length and of one tenth of the free length nearly coincide: the factor is 1 at zero load, falls below 1 in tension and rises without bound as the compressive load approaches buckling. At the ends of the free length the factor changes much less. A narrow band around 1 marks where the superposition formula, which assumes the factor is 1, is within 5 percent.

## fig5_combined (.svg / .png)
**Figure 5. Combined loads on a tube (Beer §8.3, preview of Lab 8)**

Three parts. (a) Side view of a tube fixed to a wall at the left. A lever arm at the free end points toward the viewer, and a downward force F at its tip bends and twists the tube. A section is cut a distance L b from the lever; point H is at the top of that section. (b, left) The section seen end-on, with H at the top and K at the side, on the neutral axis. (b, right) Two square stress elements on the tube surface. At H there is a normal stress from bending, M c over I, along the tube, plus a shearing stress from torsion, T c over J. At K the bending stress is zero and only the axial stress P over A remains, while the shearing stress is the torsional stress plus or minus the transverse-shear stress V Q over I t.
