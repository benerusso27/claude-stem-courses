Where the course goes

Fluid Mechanics I is built on one idea from physics: energy is conserved, unless something takes it away. Every chapter after this one adds a piece to that statement.

Slide 4 writes the whole course in one line. For a chunk of fluid with volume \(V\) moving at speed \(v\) at height \(h\):

\[ \tfrac12 m v^2 + m g h + p V = \text{constant} \qquad \text{(if dissipation is negligible)} \]

Kinetic energy, potential energy, and the energy stored as pressure. Divide by \(V\) and you get Bernoulli's equation (Chapter 6). Add pumps, turbines and friction and you get the energy equation (Chapter 7). Chapters 8 to 11 are about the friction term, Chapter 13 about the pump term. Chapter 16 switches from energy to momentum, and Chapter 17 applies it to drag and lift.

The elective Fluid Mechanics II works with \(F = ma\) for every fluid particle, the Navier–Stokes equation (slide 4). You don't need it here, but the viscous term \(\mu\nabla^2 V\) in it is the same viscosity you meet in Chapter 2.

Liquids or gases? Slide 3 separates them by compressibility. Liquids barely change volume under pressure, so we treat them as incompressible: density is constant. Gases compress easily. In this course, gases appear only where their density hardly changes (air in a manometer, air flowing slowly over a car), so the same equations work.

Mass, weight and the slug

\(F = ma\) ties force to mass, so only one of the two can be a base unit (slide 8). SI picks mass: 1 N is the force that accelerates 1 kg at 1 m/s². The US system picks force: 1 slug is the mass that 1 lb accelerates at 1 ft/s². So

\[ 1\ \text{N} = 1\ \text{kg·m/s}^2, \qquad 1\ \text{slug} = 1\ \text{lb·s}^2/\text{ft}. \]

The pound-mass (lbm) is a practical unit: the mass that weighs 1 lb on Earth. Since \(w = mg\), 1 lbm = 1 lb ÷ 32.2 ft/s² = 1/32.2 slug. Mixing lbm into \(F = ma\) without that 32.2 is the classic mistake, and it is why Example 1.8 says the "cheap answer" 1.041 lbm is not what we want.

Consistent units (Mott §1.7): put every quantity in base units (m, kg, s, N or ft, slug, s, lb) before you substitute. Then the answer comes out in base units automatically, and you only convert at the end.

Density, specific weight, specific gravity

In fluids you rarely follow one fixed chunk of mass. You describe how mass and weight are spread through space (slide 12), so these three properties replace m and W.

Density ρ (rho)

\(\rho = m/V\). Mass per volume. kg/m³ or slug/ft³. Water: 1000 kg/m³, 1.94 slug/ft³.

Specific weight γ (gamma)

\(\gamma = W/V = \rho g\). Weight per volume. N/m³ or lb/ft³. Water: 9.81 kN/m³, 62.4 lb/ft³.

Specific gravity sg

\(sg = \rho/\rho_w = \gamma/\gamma_w\), water at 4 °C. No units, same number in both systems.

Why γ = ρg. Start from \(\gamma = W/V\) and substitute \(W = mg\): \(\gamma = mg/V = (m/V)\,g = \rho g\). Specific weight shows up whenever gravity does the work, so it is the property you will use most in statics (\(\Delta p = \gamma h\)) and in the energy equation (\(p/\gamma\)).

CH 1 slides 16–21 · Mott §1.8, §3.2

Pressure

Force per unit area, acting perpendicular to every surface the fluid touches. Chapter 3 is all about how it varies with depth. Here you need the definition and the two reference points.

\(p = F/A\). In a fluid at rest there is no shear stress, so the only force a fluid can put on a wall is a push straight into it. That is why pressure acts normal to any surface (slide 18).

What pressure really is. Molecules in a liquid or gas move constantly and bounce off the walls. Each impact is tiny, but there are a lot of them: a cube of water 1 µm on a side holds about 3×1010 molecules (slide 18 says about 60 billion; 33 billion is closer, but the point is the same). The averaged push of all those impacts is the pressure.

Absolute, gauge and vacuum

Absolute pressure is measured from a perfect vacuum. Gauge pressure is measured from the local atmosphere, which is what most gauges read, because the atmosphere pushes on the gauge's other side. So

\[ p_{abs} = p_{gage} + p_{atm}, \qquad \text{psia} = \text{psig} + 14.7 \ \text{(at sea level)} \]

A negative gauge pressure is a partial vacuum. Absolute pressure can never be negative.

Viscosity

How strongly a fluid resists being sheared while it flows. Stirring honey takes more effort than stirring water at the same speed, and viscosity puts a number on that difference.

Solid versus fluid

Push sideways on the top of a solid block (slide 4). It leans over by a fixed amount and stops: shear stress is proportional to shear strain, \(\tau = G\gamma_s\), with \(G\) the shear modulus. Do the same to a fluid and it never stops deforming while the force acts. There is no shear modulus. What a fluid resists is the rate of deformation.

Newton's law of viscosity

Put fluid between a stationary plate and a plate moving at speed \(v\) (slide 5). The fluid sticks to both plates (the no-slip condition), so its velocity goes from 0 at the bottom to \(v\) at the top. The velocity gradient \(dv/dy\) is the rate at which the fluid is being sheared. For most common fluids, the shear stress is proportional to it:

\[ \tau = \eta\,\frac{dv}{dy} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \eta = \frac{\tau}{dv/dy} \]

The constant \(\eta\) (eta) is the dynamic viscosity. Many other books call it \(\mu\) (slide 6). Fluids that obey this law are Newtonian: water, oils, gasoline, air. This course uses only Newtonian fluids (slide 11).

Units. \(\eta = \tau/(dv/dy)\) has units of (N/m²)/(1/s) = N·s/m² = Pa·s = kg/(m·s). US: lb·s/ft² = slug/(ft·s). The older poise is 0.1 Pa·s, and the centipoise (cP) is 1 mPa·s, about the viscosity of water at 20 °C.

Kinematic viscosity (Mott §2.3) is viscosity divided by density, \(\nu = \eta/\rho\), in m²/s or ft²/s (1 centistoke = 1 mm²/s). It will matter in the Reynolds number in Chapter 8.

Slide 7: the block on an oil film

A block of base area \(A\) slides on an oil film of thickness \(h\), pulled by a hanging mass \(m\). At steady speed nothing accelerates, so the hanging weight balances the viscous drag on the block's base:

\[ m g = \tau A = \eta\,\frac{v}{h}\,A \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad v = \frac{m g h}{\eta A} \]

The block's own mass \(M\) doesn't appear. It only matters while the system speeds up. Try it in the shear cell: the force \(F\) there is the \(mg\) needed to hold that plate speed.

Viscosity and temperature

Viscosity is the most temperature-sensitive property you will use. Always look it up at the fluid's actual temperature.

Trap

Slide 8 says viscosity drops as temperature rises. That is true for liquids. For gases it is the opposite: air gets more viscous when heated. Switch the chart to air to see it. In a liquid, heat loosens the attraction between molecules. In a gas, heat makes molecules move faster and carry more momentum between layers.

Viscosity index

The viscosity index (VI) rates how much an oil's viscosity changes with temperature. A high VI means a small change, which is what you want in an engine or a hydraulic system that runs from cold starts to hot operation (Mott §2.5).

CH2 s7Block pulled across an oil filmSteady speed of the large massv = mgh/(ηA)
  1. Steady motion means no acceleration, so forces on each body balance. The string tension equals the hanging weight, \(T = mg\).
  2. On the block, the tension is balanced by the shear stress on its base: \(T = \tau A\).
  3. The oil film is thin, so the velocity profile is linear: \(\tau = \eta v/h\).
  4. \(mg = \eta v A/h\), so \(v = mgh/(\eta A)\).

Numbers to try: \(m\) = 0.5 kg, \(h\) = 0.5 mm, SAE 30-like oil \(\eta\) = 0.3 Pa·s, \(A\) = 0.04 m². Then \(v = (0.5)(9.81)(0.0005)/(0.3 \times 0.04) = 0.20\) m/s.

Self-test

Check yourself

Pick an answer. The page tells you whether it's right and why.

1. An object weighs 64.4 lb on Earth. What is its mass?

\(m = W/g = 64.4/32.2 = 2.0\) slug. It is also 64.4 lbm, but not 2.0 lbm.

2. A liquid has sg = 1.26. Its specific weight in US units is

\(\gamma = sg \times 62.4 = 78.6\) lb/ft³. Option b is its density, not its specific weight.

3. A tire gauge reads 32 psi in Denver, where the atmosphere is about 12.2 psia. The absolute pressure in the tire is

Gauge pressure is measured from the local atmosphere: 32 + 12.2 = 44.2 psia. 46.7 uses the sea-level 14.7.

4. You double the gap between the plates in a shear cell but keep the plate speed. The force to move the plate

\(F = \eta (v/h) A\). Doubling \(h\) halves the velocity gradient, so it halves τ and F.

5. Which is true of a fluid at rest?

Any shear stress would make a fluid flow. At rest, only normal stress (pressure) is possible. This is CH 3's opening pop question.

6. Heating air from 20 °C to 80 °C makes its dynamic viscosity

Mott Table E.1: 1.81×10⁻⁵ to 2.09×10⁻⁵ Pa·s, about +15%. Gases get more viscous when heated.

Why does a fluid have no shear modulus, and what replaces it?answer

A solid's molecules are locked to their neighbours, so a shear strain stores elastic energy and the stress stops growing once the strain matches it. A fluid's molecules slide past each other freely, so any steady shear stress produces continuing deformation. What a fluid resists is how fast it deforms, so the material constant relates stress to strain rate: \(\tau = \eta\,dv/dy\).

Show that the units of η are kg/(m·s).answer

\(\eta = \tau/(dv/dy)\). τ is N/m² = kg/(m·s²). \(dv/dy\) is (m/s)/m = 1/s. Dividing: kg/(m·s²) × s = kg/(m·s).

When is it fair to treat a gas as incompressible?answer

When its density changes by only a few percent in the problem, which is the case when pressure changes are small compared with the absolute pressure, and speeds are well below the speed of sound (roughly under 100 m/s for air). Air in a manometer tube or flowing over a car qualifies. Air in a compressor does not.
