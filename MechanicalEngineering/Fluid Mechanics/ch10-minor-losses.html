Minor Losses

Every valve, elbow, entrance, exit and change of size disturbs the flow, and the eddies it makes turn energy into heat. Each one costs \(h_L = K\,v^2/2g\). "Minor" is a name, not a promise (slide 2): in a short line full of fittings they can be most of the loss.

The resistance coefficient

A straight pipe loses \(f\,\dfrac{L}{D}\,\dfrac{v^2}{2g}\). A fitting is described the same way, with one number standing in for \(fL/D\):

\[ h_L = K\,\frac{v^2}{2g} \]

\(K\), the resistance or loss coefficient, is dimensionless, like \(f\) and \(L/D\), so it's the same in SI and US units (slide 3). Add every loss along the line (slide 4):

\[ h_L = \sum_i f_i\frac{L_i}{D_i}\frac{v_i^2}{2g} + \sum_j K_j\frac{v_j^2}{2g} \quad\xrightarrow{\text{one diameter}}\quad h_L = \left(f\frac{L}{D} + \sum K\right)\frac{v^2}{2g} \]

Slide 6: how to treat these formulas

  • None of them is derived from first principles; they are fits to experiments. You don't need to memorize them, but expect qualitative questions on how K behaves.
  • The course assumes fully turbulent flow, where K (and f) barely change with NR.
  • Always check which velocity a K is defined with. Enlargements and exits use the upstream (fast) velocity; contractions and entrances use the downstream (fast) velocity. Either way it's the larger velocity.

Why the losses happen (slide 14's video): whenever the flow has to change direction or speed abruptly, it separates from the wall and forms recirculating eddies. Keeping those eddies spinning is where the energy goes.

Each fitting, in turn

s7Exit into a tankPipe discharging into a large reservoirK = 1

All the pipe's velocity head is spent: the jet mixes into still water and its kinetic energy becomes heat. Writing it as \(\dfrac{p_1}{\gamma} + \dfrac{v_1^2}{2g} - K\dfrac{v_1^2}{2g} = \dfrac{p_2}{\gamma}\), the tank pressure \(p_2\) equals the pipe pressure \(p_1\) only if \(K = 1\). That's what experiments show: none of the velocity head is recovered as pressure (the 2022 midterm problem below).

s8Sudden enlargementSmall pipe D1 into large pipe D2; K uses v1K ≈ [1 − (D1/D2)²]²

Some velocity head is recovered as pressure (the flow slows to \(v_2\)), but the jet's mixing wastes the rest. Momentum (Chapter 16) gives \(K = [1 - (A_1/A_2)]^2\). Mott's Table 10.1A shows the measured K is a little lower at high velocity and higher at low velocity; the explorer plots both. As \(D_2/D_1 \to \infty\), K → 1: an exit into a tank is the limit.

s9Sudden contractionLarge into small; K uses v2K ≈ 0.4–0.5 for big ratios

The flow overshoots into a vena contracta narrower than the small pipe, then re-expands to fill it. The loss is mostly that re-expansion, so it's much smaller than a sudden enlargement's for the same ratio.

Slide 9 writes \(K \approx 0.42[1 - (D_2/D_1)^2]^2\). The form I've seen elsewhere (White's Fluid Mechanics) is \(0.42[1 - (D_2/D_1)^2]\) with no outer square. I don't know which one your instructor intends. Mott's measured Table 10.3A falls between the two, and the explorer shows all three, so check with your instructor if a number depends on it.

s10Gradual enlargement (diffuser)K uses v1 and depends on D1/D2 and the cone angleθ ≈ 7–20° is best

A gentle cone lets the flow slow down without separating, so more velocity head comes back as pressure. Slide 10's figure (θ = 20°) lists K = 0.30, 0.25, 0.15, 0.10 for d/D = 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8. Too wide an angle and it acts like a sudden enlargement; too narrow and the cone is so long that wall friction adds up (Mott Fig. 10.5 has the full curves).

A diffuser built to raise pressure is rated by its recovery factor, \(\Delta p_{real}/\Delta p_{ideal}\), with

\[ \Delta p_{ideal} = \frac{\gamma(v_1^2 - v_2^2)}{2g},\qquad \Delta p_{real} = \gamma\left[\frac{v_1^2 - v_2^2}{2g} - h_L\right] \]

Slide 10 writes the real one as \(\gamma(v_1^2 - v_2^2 - h_L)/2g\), which subtracts a length from a velocity squared. \(h_L\) belongs outside the fraction, as above.

s11Gradual contraction (reducer)β = D2/D1, included angle θsmall K: 0.02–0.2 typical

The formulas on slide 11 are the Crane Technical Paper 410 correlations:

\[ K = \frac{0.8\sin(\theta/2)(1-\beta^2)}{\beta^4}\ (\theta \le 45°),\qquad K = \frac{0.5\sqrt{\sin(\theta/2)}\,(1-\beta^2)}{\beta^4}\ (45° < \theta \le 180°) \]

Dividing by β⁴ converts K to the large pipe's velocity (Crane gives both forms). Slide 11 pairs them with \(v_2^2/2g\), the small pipe's velocity, which overstates the loss by \(1/\beta^4\) (16× for β = 0.5). With \(v_2\), use \(K = 0.8\sin(\theta/2)(1 - \beta^2)\) without the β⁴. Check: at θ = 180° the small-pipe form gives \(0.5(1 - \beta^2)\), the familiar sudden-contraction value. The explorer shows both.

s12Entrance from a tankK uses the pipe velocity0.8, 0.5, 0.2, 0.04

A pipe sticking into the tank (re-entrant) is worst, then a sharp square edge (0.5), then slightly and well-rounded inlets. Mott's Fig. 10.14 lists 1.0 for an inward-projecting pipe where the slide uses 0.8; both are in use, so use whichever your instructor specifies.

Back to the leaking tub (slide 12): a sharp-edged hole has K ≈ 0.5, so the energy equation gives \(h = (1 + K)v^2/2g\), \(v = \sqrt{2gh/1.5} = 0.82\sqrt{2gh}\). Torricelli overestimates the speed by about 20 % for a sharp hole and 2 % for a well-rounded one.

s13BendsK depends on the bend radius R/d and roughnessminimum near R/d ≈ 3–5

The fluid on the outside of a bend is pushed outward, setting up a pair of swirling secondary flows. A tight bend separates; a very gentle one is long, adding friction, so K has a minimum around R/d of 3 to 5. A bend is one of the few fittings where roughness clearly matters (slide 13's chart). Mott handles bends with equivalent lengths (§10.11).

Equivalent length, and valves

With one diameter, \(h_L = (fL/D + K)\,v^2/2g\). Pretend the fitting is extra pipe:

\[ h_L = f\,\frac{L + L_e}{D}\,\frac{v^2}{2g},\qquad L_e = \frac{KD}{f} \]

Tables give \(L_e/D\) for valves and fittings (Mott Table 10.4), and Mott's procedure turns it into K using the friction factor in the zone of complete turbulence for that pipe, \(f_T\) (Table 10.5):

\[ K = f_T\,\frac{L_e}{D} \]

Then the whole line is "one long straight pipe" (slide 21). It works best where f barely changes: fully turbulent flow in fittings the same size as the pipe.

Worked problems from the CH 10 slides

Slide examples, solved

s16–17Well pump with minor losses (Mott 8.34 extended)745 gal/h of 60 °F water, 140 ft of 1 in Sch 40, 120 ft lift to a 40 psig tank; add exit, entrance and elbow losseshL ≈ 17 ft; 0.72 hp
  1. From Ch 8 (slide 17): static head \(120 + 40(144)/62.4 = 212.3\) ft; \(Q = 0.0277\) ft³/s, \(D = 1.049\) in, \(v = 4.61\) ft/s, \(N_R = 3.3\times10^4\).
  2. The slide uses \(D/\varepsilon \approx 250\) and \(f \approx 0.03\) (Colebrook gives 0.031), so the pipe loses \(0.03 \times \dfrac{140}{0.0874} \times 0.330 = 15.9 \approx 16\) ft.
  3. Minor losses: \(v^2/2g = 0.33\) ft, \(K_{exit} = 1\), \(K_{entrance} = 0.8\), \(K_{bend} = 1\): \(2.8 \times 0.33 = 0.92 \approx 1\) ft.
  4. Total ≈ 17 ft, \(h_A \approx 229\) ft, \(P_A = h_A\gamma Q = 229(62.4)(0.0277) = 396\) ft·lb/s = 0.72 hp. Here the minor losses are small because the pipe is long.
  5. "What if there's a valve?" A fully open globe valve: \(K = f_T(L_e/D) = 0.022 \times 340 = 7.5\), another 2.5 ft, more than all the other fittings together. A gate valve: \(K = 0.18\), negligible. Load both in the builder.
s182022 Midterm #2 · water level in a tank fed by a pipePipe at 20 kPa gauge and 3 m/s discharges into the bottom of a tank; K = 1 for the expansion2.50 m without loss; 2.04 m with it

Assumptions: the tank's air space is at atmospheric pressure, the water surface is still, and h is measured from the pipe's centerline.

  1. Bernoulli, pipe to surface: \(h = \dfrac{p}{\gamma} + \dfrac{v^2}{2g} = \dfrac{20}{9.81} + \dfrac{9}{19.62} = 2.04 + 0.46 = 2.50\) m.
  2. With the exit loss: \(h = \dfrac{p}{\gamma} + \dfrac{v^2}{2g} - (1)\dfrac{v^2}{2g} = 2.04\) m. The velocity head is lost entirely, so the level is set by the pipe's pressure alone.

Textbook problems, solved

Ex 10.1Sudden enlargement in copper tube100 L/min of water, 25 mm × 1.5 mm tube into 80 mm × 2.8 mm tubeK ≈ 0.74; hL ≈ 0.72 m
  1. Inside diameters 22.0 and 74.4 mm, \(D_2/D_1 = 3.38\). \(v_1 = Q/A_1 = 4.385\) m/s, \(v_1^2/2g = 0.980\) m.
  2. Mott's chart at 4.4 m/s gives K ≈ 0.72–0.74 (the table interpolates to 0.73), so \(h_L \approx 0.72\) m. The book reads 0.725 m. The theoretical formula gives 0.83, higher, as the book warns for high velocities.
Ex 10.5Sudden contraction, the reverseSame tubes, flow from large to smallK ≈ 0.415; hL = 0.41 m
  1. \(D_1/D_2 = 3.38\), \(v_2 = 4.385\) m/s, \(v_2^2/2g = 0.980\) m.
  2. Mott Fig. 10.8: K = 0.415, so \(h_L = 0.407\) m. Compare with 0.72 m for the enlargement: same tubes, same flow, but contracting loses much less.
Ex 10.7K of a globe valveFully open, in 6 in Sch 40 steelK = 5.10; Le = 172 ft
  1. \(L_e/D = 340\), \(f_T = 0.015\): \(K = 340(0.015) = 5.10\).
  2. \(L_e = (L_e/D)D = 340(0.5054\text{ ft}) = 172\) ft of pipe.
Ex 10.8Pressure drop across a globe valve4 in Sch 40, 400 gal/min of oil, sg 0.87hL = 8.6 ft; Δp = 3.2 psi
  1. \(K = 340 \times 0.016 = 5.44\). \(Q = 400/449 = 0.891\) ft³/s, \(v = 0.891/0.0884 = 10.1\) ft/s, \(v^2/2g = 1.58\) ft.
  2. \(h_L = 5.44 \times 1.58 = 8.6\) ft; \(\Delta p = \gamma h_L = 0.87(62.4)(8.6)/144 = 3.2\) psi.

Self-test (slide 6: expect qualitative questions)

Check yourself

1. Same two pipe sizes, same flow rate. Which loses more energy?

The enlargement's jet mixing is far more wasteful (Ex 10.1 vs 10.5: 0.72 m vs 0.41 m).

2. The K for a pipe exit into a large tank is about

All the velocity head is lost in the tank.

Globe valves (L_e/D = 340) throttle and seal well; gate and ball valves are for full open or closed.

4. Rounding the entrance of a pipe from a tank changes K from 0.5 to 0.04. The total head loss of a long line

In the slide 16 well pump, the entrance is under 2 % of the loss. Minor losses matter most in short lines with many fittings.
