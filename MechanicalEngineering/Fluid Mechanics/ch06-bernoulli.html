Continuity and Bernoulli

The fluid starts moving. Two conservation laws carry this chapter: mass in equals mass out (continuity), and with no losses the energy per unit weight stays constant along the flow (Bernoulli). Energy just trades places among pressure, elevation and velocity.

Slides 6–8 · Mott §6.3

Continuity: what goes in comes out

In steady flow nothing piles up inside a pipe, so the mass flow rate is the same at every section:

\[ \rho_1 A_1 v_1 = \rho_2 A_2 v_2 \]

A liquid's density hardly changes, so \(\rho\) cancels:

\[ A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2 \quad\text{or}\quad Q_1 = Q_2 \]

Halve the diameter and the area drops to a quarter, so the velocity goes up four times. With branches (slide 8), the flows add: \(Q_1 = Q_2 + Q_3\). Gases need the full \(\rho A v\) version because their density changes with pressure.

Slides 9–14 · Mott §6.6–6.8

Bernoulli's equation is energy bookkeeping

Slides 9–11 start with a ball thrown upward: it trades speed for height and back, because (ignoring air drag) its total energy is constant. A water jet does the same. In a pipe, the fluid has a third place to store energy: pressure.

  1. 1

    Three energies of a fluid element of weight w

    Potential \(wz\); kinetic \(wv^2/2g\); and flow energy \(wp/\gamma\), the work done pushing the element into the section against pressure \(p\) (Mott §6.6).

  2. 2

    No losses, no pumps: total is constant

    \[ w\frac{p_1}{\gamma} + wz_1 + w\frac{v_1^2}{2g} = w\frac{p_2}{\gamma} + wz_2 + w\frac{v_2^2}{2g} \]
  3. 3

    Divide by w: energy per unit weight = head

    \[ \frac{p_1}{\gamma} + z_1 + \frac{v_1^2}{2g} = \frac{p_2}{\gamma} + z_2 + \frac{v_2^2}{2g} \]

    Each term is a length: pressure head, elevation head, velocity head; their sum is the total head. Multiply by \(\gamma\) for the per-unit-volume form on slide 12: static pressure + \(\rho g z\) + dynamic pressure \(\rho v^2/2\). Same equation, two habits: academia per volume, industry per weight ("head").

Slide 12: the assumptions, and what breaks them

  • Steady flow between the two points. A draining tank isn't steady, but changes slowly enough (slide 16) to treat as steady moment by moment.
  • Incompressible: liquids, and gases at low speed (below roughly a third of the speed of sound).
  • No energy losses to friction. Real pipes lose energy, and Chapter 7 adds a loss term \(h_L\).
  • No pumps, turbines or heat transfer between the points. Chapter 7 adds \(h_A\) and \(h_R\).
  • Same streamline (or the same continuous flow). You can't Bernoulli across a pump or between two separate pipes.

Mott's procedure

  1. Pick two points where you know the most (free surfaces: \(p = 0\), \(v \approx 0\); free jets: \(p = 0\)).
  2. Write the full equation, then cross out terms that are zero or equal.
  3. Use continuity for the second unknown velocity.
  4. Solve, then check units: every term must be in metres or feet.
Trap

Slides 15–17 · Mott §6.9–6.10

A leaking tub: Torricelli and the draining tank

Point 1 on the free surface (p = 0, v ≈ 0), point 2 in the jet (p = 0) a depth h below. Bernoulli leaves \(h = v^2/2g\), so \(v = \sqrt{2gh}\): the speed of an object dropped from height h.

  1. 1

    Speed and rate of loss

    \[ v_j = \sqrt{2gh}, \qquad Q = A_j\sqrt{2gh} \]
  2. 2

    Rate of change of height

    The volume leaving equals the volume lost from the tub: \(A_t\,dh = -Q\,dt\), so

    \[ \frac{dh}{dt} = -\frac{A_j}{A_t}\sqrt{2gh} \]

    Slide 16's "quite close because…": the tub is much bigger than the hole, so the surface moves very slowly and its velocity head is negligible. That's what lets us use the steady Bernoulli equation.

  3. 3

    Time to drain (slide 17, Mott Eq. 6–26)

    Separate and integrate from \(h_1\) to \(h_2\):

    \[ t_2 - t_1 = \frac{2(A_t/A_j)}{\sqrt{2g}}\left(\sqrt{h_1} - \sqrt{h_2}\right) \]

    With \(h_2 = 0\) this is the slide's \(t_{drain}\). The last bit of water takes the longest: it takes as long to drain the bottom quarter of the depth as the top three quarters.

Slides 23–25 · Mott §6.8, Example 6.11

Measuring speed with pressure: pitot tube and venturi

Pitot tube (slide 23). A tube facing upstream brings the fluid at its tip to rest. Bernoulli from the free stream to that stagnation point: \(p_s + \rho v^2/2 = p_t\). Measure the total (stagnation) pressure and the static pressure, and

\[ v = \sqrt{\frac{2(p_t - p_s)}{\rho}} \]

It works inside a pipe or on an airplane, which is why it shows up on F1 cars.

Venturi (slides 24–25). A smooth throat speeds the flow up and drops its pressure. Bernoulli gives one equation with two unknown velocities; continuity, \(A_1v_1 = A_2v_2\), is the second equation the slide says we need. With a differential manometer of deflection h and gauge fluid \(\gamma_g\), the elevation terms cancel (Mott Ex 6.11):

\[ \frac{v_2^2 - v_1^2}{2g} = h\left(\frac{\gamma_g}{\gamma} - 1\right),\qquad v_1 = \sqrt{\frac{2gh(\gamma_g/\gamma - 1)}{(A_1/A_2)^2 - 1}} \]

Real venturis lose a little energy; Chapter 15 adds a discharge coefficient (about 0.98). This page uses the ideal value, as Ex 6.11 does.

Slides 26–27

The Magnus effect, briefly

A spinning ball drags air around with it. On the side where the surface moves with the oncoming air, the flow is faster; on the other side it is slower. Faster flow, lower pressure, so the ball is pushed toward the fast side and curves. The slide is careful: Bernoulli explains only part of it. The spinning surface also deflects the wake to one side, and by Newton's third law the ball is pushed the other way (the Coanda effect, which returns in Chapter 16). Strictly, Bernoulli also needs the two streams to come from the same undisturbed flow upstream, which is a fair approximation here.

Slide examples, solved

s282017 Midterm · thumb over a garden hoseExit speed 1 m/s; the thumb blocks 80 % of the outlet; p at the outlet was 0 gauge≈ 12 kPa rise
  1. Continuity, assuming the same flow rate: the open area drops to 20 %, so the exit speed rises 5×, to 5 m/s.
  2. Bernoulli from just inside the hose (velocity 1 m/s, pressure p) to the exit jet (5 m/s, 0 gauge), same height: \(p = \tfrac{\rho}{2}(v_2^2 - v_1^2) = \tfrac{1000}{2}(25 - 1) = 12{,}000\) Pa.

The "estimate" is in the assumption: a real faucet doesn't hold Q constant when you block the outlet, so the true flow drops a little and the pressure rise is a little less than 12 kPa. Note the fraction: covering 80 % leaves 20 % open, not 80 %.

s15–17Leaking bathtubOriginal numbers: tub 1.5 m × 0.70 m, 0.30 m of water, 40 mm drain2.43 m/s; ≈ 3.1 L/s; ≈ 1.5 min
  1. Leak speed: \(v = \sqrt{2(9.81)(0.30)} = 2.43\) m/s.
  2. Rate of loss: \(A_j = \pi(0.04)^2/4 = 1.257\times10^{-3}\) m², so \(Q = 3.05\times10^{-3}\) m³/s ≈ 3.1 L/s.
  3. Height falls at \(dh/dt = -Q/A_t = -3.05\times10^{-3}/1.05 = -2.9\) mm/s at the start.
  4. Drain time: \(t = \dfrac{2(1.05/1.257\times10^{-3})}{\sqrt{19.62}}\sqrt{0.30} = 207\) s with a rounded outlet. A real drain grate is closer to a sharp orifice: dividing by 0.62 gives about 330 s, 5½ min. Try it in the draining-tank tool (Bathtub).
s8Practice · one inlet, two outletsSlide 8 has no numbersQ1 = Q2 + Q3

Continuity for a liquid with several openings: flow in equals the sum of flows out, \(A_1v_1 = A_2v_2 + A_3v_3\). The continuity tool uses made-up numbers to show it.

s22Siphon (Mott Ex 6.10)40 mm tube, 25 mm nozzle; crest 1.2 m above the surface, outlet 3.0 m belowQ = 3.77 L/s; pC = −16.3 kPa
  1. A to F (both 0 gauge, \(v_A = 0\)): \(v_F = \sqrt{2g(3.0)} = 7.67\) m/s, \(Q = 7.67 \times \pi(0.025)^2/4 = 3.77\times10^{-3}\) m³/s.
  2. In the 40 mm tube: \(v = Q/A = 3.00\) m/s, velocity head 0.459 m.
  3. Bernoulli from A to each point: \(p = \gamma(z_A - z - v^2/2g)\). B (level with A): −4.50 kPa. C (1.2 m up): −16.27 kPa. D: −4.50 kPa. E (3.0 m down): +24.93 kPa.

Point B is at the same level as the pool surface but below atmospheric pressure: some of the pressure head became velocity head.

6.60Exercise #1 · gasoline through a reducersg 0.67, 0.11 m³/s, 415 kPa in the 160 mm OD × 5.5 mm wall tube; find p in the 80 mm OD × 2.8 mm wall tube≈ 214 kPa
  1. Inside diameters: 160 − 2(5.5) = 149.0 mm and 80 − 2(2.8) = 74.4 mm. Areas \(1.744\times10^{-2}\) and \(4.347\times10^{-3}\) m² (match Mott's Appendix G tube table to 3 figures).
  2. \(v_1 = 0.11/0.01744 = 6.31\) m/s, \(v_2 = 25.3\) m/s.
  3. Same elevation: \(p_2 = p_1 - \tfrac{\rho}{2}(v_2^2 - v_1^2) = 415 - \tfrac{670}{2}(640.2 - 39.8)/1000 = 415 - 201 = 214\) kPa.

The book answer for this even-numbered problem isn't in the back of the book, so this is my own solution. The OD × wall tube sizes are a common slip: use the inside diameter.

6.61Exercise #2 · water flowing up a widening pipe10 °C, 0.37 m³/s; 300 mm at A, 600 mm at B, 4.5 m higher; pA = 66.2 kPa34.9 kPa
  1. \(v_A = 0.37/0.0707 = 5.23\) m/s, \(v_B = 1.31\) m/s. Velocity heads 1.396 m and 0.087 m.
  2. \(p_B = p_A + \gamma\left[(z_A - z_B) + \dfrac{v_A^2 - v_B^2}{2g}\right] = 66.2 + 9.81(-4.5 + 1.309) = 34.9\) kPa. Matches the book.

Slowing down gives back 1.31 m of head, but climbing costs 4.5 m: the pressure drops overall.

6.77Exercise #3 · oil flow from a manometer readingOil 8.64 kN/m³; 100 mm at A, 50 mm at B 600 mm higher; water manometer: 200 mm deflection, its higher water level 150 mm below A1.48 × 10⁻³ m³/s
  1. Manometer, from A down the oil-filled left leg to the water, across, and up the right leg through oil to B: \(p_A + \gamma_o(0.35) - \gamma_w(0.20) - \gamma_o(0.75) = p_B\), so \(p_A - p_B = 8.64(0.40) + 9.81(0.20) = 5.42\) kPa.
  2. Bernoulli A to B: \(\dfrac{p_A - p_B}{\gamma_o} - 0.60 = \dfrac{v_B^2 - v_A^2}{2g}\), so \(0.627 - 0.600 = 0.0271\) m.
  3. Continuity: \(v_B = 4v_A\), so \(15v_A^2/2g = 0.0271\), \(v_A = 0.188\) m/s.
  4. \(Q = 0.188 \times \pi(0.1)^2/4 = 1.48\times10^{-3}\) m³/s. Matches the book.

The manometer distances are read from the slide's figure. Notice how small the velocity-head difference is (27 mm): small errors in reading the figure change Q a lot.

Self-test

Check yourself

1. Water flows from a 100 mm pipe into a 50 mm pipe. The velocity in the small pipe is

v ∝ 1/A ∝ 1/D². Halving D quarters A.

2. A tank's outlet jet leaves at 6.0 m/s. If the water depth is quadrupled, the jet speed becomes

v = √(2gh): 4× the depth, 2× the speed.

3. In a siphon, raising the crest (while it still flows) changes

Q depends only on the drop from the free surface to the outlet. A higher crest means a lower crest pressure, until it reaches the vapor pressure and the siphon breaks.

4. Bernoulli's equation can't be used directly between

A pump adds energy (h_A); that is the general energy equation of Chapter 7.
