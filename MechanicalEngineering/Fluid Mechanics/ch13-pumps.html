Pump Selection

A centrifugal pump doesn't decide its own flow rate. Its curve says how much head it can give at each flow; the piping says how much head it needs. The pump runs where the two curves cross, and choosing a pump means putting that crossing where you want it, near the pump's best efficiency, with enough suction pressure to avoid cavitation.

Slides 2–3 · review of Chapter 7

What a pump does

A pump delivers a flow rate Q against a total head \(h_A\). From the energy equation between the suction and discharge sides,

\[ h_A = \frac{p_2 - p_1}{\gamma} + (z_2 - z_1) + \frac{v_2^2 - v_1^2}{2g} + h_L \]

Then the power added to the fluid is \(P_A = h_A\gamma Q\), and with mechanical efficiency \(e_M\) the motor must supply \(P_I = P_A/e_M\). Pump catalogs give that input power as BHP, brake horsepower (slide 17).

Slides 4–11, 14 · Mott §13.3–13.5

Positive-displacement vs kinetic

Positive displacement (gear, lobe, screw, piston)

Each revolution traps and pushes out a fixed volume, so Q is set by the speed and barely changes with pressure: the head–capacity line is nearly vertical (slide 10).

  • Pros: accurate flow; can make very high pressure; handles viscous fluids.
  • Cons: pulsating flow; sensitive to particles; a blocked outlet keeps building pressure until something breaks, so it needs a relief valve.

Kinetic (centrifugal: radial, mixed, axial)

An impeller throws fluid outward, adding velocity that the casing turns into pressure. Head falls as flow rises, and the flow depends on what it's pumping against (slide 11).

  • Pros: large, smooth flow at fair pressure; simple; tolerant of a closed valve for short periods.
  • Cons: large flows can cavitate at the suction; needs priming (can't pump air); doesn't guarantee a flow rate.
  • Axial (propeller): low head, huge flow. Radial: high head, moderate flow (slide 15).

Reading a pump performance chart

Designation: a "3 × 4 − 10" pump has a 3 in discharge, a 4 in suction and a casing that holds impellers up to 10 in (slide 12). The suction is bigger so that its velocity, and the pressure drop entering the pump, stay low, which helps against cavitation.

On a complete chart (like Mott Fig. 13.28 on slides 13 and 20) you'll find, for one speed:

  • head–capacity curves, one per impeller diameter;
  • efficiency contours (islands) with the peak, the best efficiency point (BEP), in the middle;
  • dashed lines of constant input power (hp);
  • a separate strip of NPSH required against capacity.

Two-pole and four-pole motors: on 60 Hz power they run near 3500 and 1750 rpm, which is why charts come at those speeds.

How to use one

  1. Given Q and h (slide 19, on the exam): find the point; pick the smallest impeller whose curve lies on or above it; read efficiency, power and NPSHr there.
  2. Given a system curve (also on the exam): the operating point is where it crosses the chosen impeller's curve.
  3. Given only the piping: you'd compute the system curve point by point (Chapters 8–11) first. That's the "real problem" of slide 23, beyond hand calculation for the exam.
Remember

A pump with a bigger impeller than needed will run at a higher flow than you asked for, or need throttling, which wastes energy. Aim for the BEP.

Slides 19–23 · Mott §13.12–13.13

System curve and selection

For the piping, apply the energy equation at a range of flow rates. At zero flow the pump must still supply the static head \(h_o\): the elevation rise plus any pressure difference between the two surfaces. Every loss and velocity head grows with \(v^2\), so

\[ h_{system} \approx h_o + KQ^2 \]

a parabola starting at \(h_o\) (slide 21). Close a valve and K grows: the curve steepens and the operating point slides left to less flow and more head. Slow the pump or trim the impeller and the pump curve drops instead. Try both in the simulator at the top; watch the efficiency.

Slides 24–28 · Mott §13.9–13.10

NPSH: keeping the suction from boiling

Inside the impeller eye the pressure drops below the suction pressure. If it reaches the liquid's vapor pressure, bubbles form and then collapse violently further in: cavitation (Chapter 7, slides 28–29).

  • NPSHR (required): from the manufacturer. The head above vapor pressure the pump needs at its inlet. It rises with flow.
  • NPSHA (available): what your suction piping actually provides (Mott Eq. 13–13):
\[ \text{NPSH}_A = h_{sp} \pm h_s - h_f - h_{vp} \]

\(h_{sp}\) = absolute pressure head on the supply surface; \(h_s\) = elevation of that surface above the pump inlet (+ if above, − if the pump lifts from below: the slide warns that this sign is the opposite of the usual habit); \(h_f\) = suction-line losses; \(h_{vp}\) = vapor pressure head.

Slide 25's blank: NPSHR should be less than NPSHA. Mott (and the Hydraulic Institute) ask for a margin: NPSHA > 1.10 NPSHR.

Slides 29–31 · Mott §13.7

Affinity laws

For one centrifugal pump, changing the speed N (diameter fixed) scales its whole curve (slide 29):

\[ \frac{Q_1}{Q_2} = \frac{N_1}{N_2},\qquad \frac{h_{a1}}{h_{a2}} = \left(\frac{N_1}{N_2}\right)^2,\qquad \frac{P_1}{P_2} = \left(\frac{N_1}{N_2}\right)^3 \]

The caveat on the slide matters: the two points compared must be at the same efficiency (corresponding points), not the same flow.

Changing the impeller diameter at fixed speed, slide 30 gives \(Q \propto D^3\), \(h \propto D^2\), \(P \propto D^5\). Mott (Eqs. 13–8 to 13–10) gives

\[ \frac{Q_1}{Q_2} = \frac{D_1}{D_2},\qquad \frac{h_{a1}}{h_{a2}} = \left(\frac{D_1}{D_2}\right)^2,\qquad \frac{P_1}{P_2} = \left(\frac{D_1}{D_2}\right)^3 \]
Slides 30–31

Both sets are real, for different situations. Mott's is for trimming the impeller in the same casing, which is what a pump catalog's impeller curves show and what Problems 13.19 and 13.20 ask. The D³/D⁵ set is for geometrically similar pumps of different size (everything scaled, including the casing). Slide 31 applies D³ to Problem 13.19, but the book's answer (capacity down 25 % for a 25 % smaller impeller) uses Mott's Q ∝ D. Ask your instructor which is expected on the exam.

Slide 15 · Mott §13.11

Specific speed: radial, mixed or axial?

\[ N_s = \frac{N\sqrt{Q}}{h^{3/4}} \]

evaluated at the best efficiency point, in Mott's US convention: N in rpm, Q in gal/min, h in ft. It isn't truly dimensionless (the units are part of the definition), so always quote it with its convention.

Low Ns (under about 2000 to 4000) means a radial impeller built for high head; high Ns (roughly 9000 to 15,000) an axial propeller built for flow; mixed-flow impellers sit between (slide 15's chart).

Self-test

Check yourself

1. A discharge valve downstream of a centrifugal pump is partly closed. The operating point moves to

The system curve steepens and meets the falling pump curve further left.

2. A positive-displacement pump's outlet is blocked. What protects it?

It keeps pushing its fixed volume; pressure rises until something gives (slide 10).

3. Pumping warmer water with the same piping, NPSHA

Vapor pressure head rises fast with temperature (0.24 m at 20 °C, 3.25 m at 70 °C).

4. Cutting a pump's speed in half (same impeller) changes its power by a factor of

P ∝ N³. That's why variable-speed drives save so much energy.
