Series and Parallel Pipes

Two rules, borrowed from electric circuits. In series, every pipe carries the same flow and the head losses add. In parallel, every branch has the same head loss and the flows add. The arithmetic is Chapters 8–10; the new part is that some problems have to be solved by iteration.

Slide 2 · Mott §11.1–11.2

Pipes in series

Nothing leaves between the ends, so continuity gives the same Q in every pipe (each with its own velocity \(v_i = Q/A_i\)). The energy equation from the upper surface to the lower one has \(p = 0\) and \(v = 0\) at both ends:

\[ H = z_1 - z_2 = \sum_i f_i\frac{L_i}{D_i}\frac{v_i^2}{2g} + \sum_j K_j\frac{v_j^2}{2g} \]

So the elevation difference is spent entirely on losses. With one diameter the sum collapses to \(\left(f\frac{L}{D} + \sum K\right)\frac{v^2}{2g}\).

The energy grade line in the diagram falls along each pipe with a slope \(h_L/L\): steepest in the smallest pipe, where the velocity is highest. Loss grows roughly with \(1/D^5\) at a fixed flow rate, so narrowing one stretch of a line is expensive.

The analogy isn't exact: head loss goes as Q² (turbulent), not Q, which is why parallel problems need iteration.

Mott §11.1–11.5

Class I, II and III problems

Mott sorts series problems by what's unknown. The slides solve a Class I problem; the tool at the top also does Class II.

Class I: find the loss

Q, pipes and fittings known. Compute each velocity, NR, f and loss directly, then solve the energy equation for a pressure, elevation or pump head. No iteration. The 2019 midterm is Class I.

Class II: find the flow

Pipes and the available head (or pressure drop) known; Q unknown. f depends on v, which depends on Q: guess f, solve for v, update f from NR, repeat. Two or three rounds usually settle it. Switch the tool to "Given H, find Q".

Class III: find the size

Q and the allowable loss known; D unknown. Guess D, compute the loss, adjust, then pick the next standard size up. Try the "One pipe" preset and change the ID until H is just met.

Slide 5 · Mott Chapter 12

Pipes in parallel

Branches that split at node 1 and rejoin at node 2 all start with the same energy and all end with the same energy. So they must lose the same head, and the flow splits itself to make that true (slide 5):

\[ h_{L,a} = h_{L,b} = \dots,\qquad Q = Q_a + Q_b + \dots \]

N branches give N unknown flows and N equations. The slides skip solving them by hand because it's time-consuming; Mott's §12.3 does two branches by writing each loss as \(h = (f L/D + \sum K)v^2/2g\), equating, and iterating on f. The solver here does the same search automatically: it finds the one head loss at which the branch flows add up to Q.

Intuition: the branch with less resistance takes more flow, but not all of it, because more flow means more loss. Close a valve in one branch and the others pick up its flow while the shared head loss rises.

Slide example, solved

s3–42019 Midterm #2 · three concrete pipes in series20 L/s of water; 1000 m of 160 mm, 1600 m of 200 mm, 850 m of 180 mm; ε = 2 mm; minor losses neglectedH ≈ 25 m
  1. Velocities: \(v = Q/(\pi D^2/4)\): 0.995, 0.637 and 0.786 m/s (the slide prints the unit as m/s², a typo for m/s).
  2. Reynolds numbers \(vD/\nu\) with \(\nu = 10^{-6}\) m²/s: 1.59 × 10⁵, 1.27 × 10⁵, 1.41 × 10⁵. Relative roughness ε/D: 0.0125, 0.010, 0.0111.
  3. Moody chart: f ≈ 0.041, 0.038, 0.038 (slide). Colebrook gives 0.0413, 0.0384, 0.0397.
  4. Losses: \(0.041\cdot\dfrac{1000}{0.16}\cdot\dfrac{0.995^2}{19.62} = 12.9\) m, then 6.3 m and 5.7 m (slide), so \(H = \sum h_L = 24.9\) m. With Colebrook's f: 13.0 + 6.3 + 5.9 = 25.2 m.

The slide labels all three losses hL,1; they are pipes 1, 2 and 3. The third f is closer to 0.040 than 0.038 at ε/D = 0.011; the difference is chart-reading precision. "Neglect minor losses" includes the exit: the jet's velocity head (0.03 m here) is too small to matter anyway. Try the preset that adds them: pipe 1 carries the entrance (0.5) and the 160→200 mm enlargement (0.13), pipe 3 the small contraction (≈ 0.04) and the exit (1.0). H rises by only about 0.06 m.

Mott's parallel example (statement paraphrased)

Textbook problem, solved

Ex 12.1Heat exchanger with a bypass100 gal/min of 60 °F water in 2 in Sch 40. Branch a: heat exchanger (K = 7.5) and two open gate valves, negligible pipe. Branch b: 20 ft of 1¼ in Sch 40, two standard elbows and an open globe valveQa ≈ 74 gal/min, Qb ≈ 26 gal/min; Δp ≈ 2.6 psi
  1. Branch a: \(K = 2(0.019)(8) + 7.5 = 7.80\), so \(h_a = 7.80\,v_a^2/2g\).
  2. Branch b: elbows \(2(0.021)(30) = 1.26\), globe valve \(0.021(340) = 7.14\), pipe \(f_b(20/0.1150) = 173.9f_b\): \(h_b = (8.40 + 173.9f_b)\,v_b^2/2g\).
  3. Equal losses and \(Q = A_av_a + A_bv_b\) with \(A_a = 0.02333\), \(A_b = 0.01039\) ft². Guess \(f_b = 0.023\), solve, check NR = 5.3 × 10⁴, update \(f_b = 0.0248\), solve again.
  4. Result: \(v_a = 7.09\) ft/s and \(v_b = 5.55\) ft/s, so \(Q_a = 0.165\) ft³/s (74 gal/min) and \(Q_b = 0.058\) ft³/s (26 gal/min).
  5. \(\Delta p = \gamma h_a = 62.4 \times 7.80 \times 7.09^2/64.4 = 380\) lb/ft² = 2.64 psi.

Mott's intermediate text swaps the labels va and vb once; the final values here match its result and the parallel solver.

Self-test

Check yourself

1. Three pipes in series carry water between two reservoirs. Which is the same in all three?

Continuity: Q is the same; velocity changes with diameter.

2. Two parallel branches rejoin. Branch a is short and wide, branch b long and narrow. Then

Same head loss across both; the easier path carries more flow, but b still carries some.

3. "Find the flow rate a 15 m drop pushes through this pipe" is

Unknown flow: f depends on N_R, which depends on the unknown velocity.
