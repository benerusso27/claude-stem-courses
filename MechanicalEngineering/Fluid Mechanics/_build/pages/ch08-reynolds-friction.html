Slides 3–14

The Reynolds number

One dimensionless number that compares the fluid's momentum with its viscosity. It predicts whether the flow is laminar or turbulent.

Where the formula comes from

A moving fluid carries momentum, and that momentum tries to keep each parcel going its own way. Viscosity drags neighbouring layers together and damps out disturbances. Turbulence starts when momentum overwhelms viscosity (slide 4). You can size each effect as a stress:

\[ \underbrace{\rho v^2}_{\text{inertial stress}} \qquad \text{vs.} \qquad \underbrace{\eta\,\frac{v}{D}}_{\text{viscous stress } \tau = \eta\,dv/dy} \]

Divide one by the other and the Reynolds number falls out:

\[ N_R = \frac{\rho v^2}{\eta v / D} = \frac{v D \rho}{\eta} = \frac{v D}{\nu}, \qquad \nu = \frac{\eta}{\rho} \]

Here \(\eta\) is the dynamic viscosity (Pa·s) and \(\nu\) is the kinematic viscosity (m²/s). All the units cancel: \(\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\cdot\text{m}\cdot\frac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3}\cdot\frac{\text{m·s}}{\text{kg}} = 1\). That is why two pipes with the same \(N_R\) behave alike even if the fluids, sizes and speeds differ (slide 7).

The thresholds for pipe flow

Your course uses these limits (slide 10):

Laminar

\(N_R < 2000\). Smooth layers, parabolic velocity profile, and \(f = 64/N_R\) exactly.

Critical zone

\(2000 \le N_R \le 4000\). The flow can flip either way, so \(f\) can't be predicted. The course skips this zone (slide 24).

Turbulent

\(N_R > 4000\). Eddies mix the flow. \(f\) depends on \(N_R\) and the pipe roughness \(\varepsilon/D\).

The main event · slides 19–20, 24

Deriving hL = f (L/D)(v²/2g)

The Darcy equation holds for laminar and turbulent flow alike. It comes from a force balance, the energy equation and one definition. The Reynolds number enters through that definition.

  1. 1

    Balance the forces on the slug

    The slug isn't accelerating, so the forces along the pipe sum to zero. Pressure acts on the end area \(\pi D^2/4\). Wall shear stress \(\tau_w\) acts on the side area \(\pi D L\) and points against the flow.

    \[ p_1\,\frac{\pi D^2}{4} \;-\; p_2\,\frac{\pi D^2}{4} \;-\; \class{k-tw}{\tau_w}\,(\pi D L) = 0 \]
  2. 2

    Solve for the pressure drop

    \[ \Delta p = p_1 - p_2 = \frac{4\,\class{k-tw}{\tau_w}\,L}{D} \]

    This already shows the shape of the answer. A longer pipe has more wall to drag on, so Δp grows with L. A wider pipe has more cross-section to push on per unit of wall, so Δp falls with D.

  3. 3

    Turn pressure into head with the energy equation

    \[ \frac{p_1}{\gamma} + z_1 + \frac{v_1^2}{2g} = \frac{p_2}{\gamma} + z_2 + \frac{v_2^2}{2g} + h_L \]

    The pipe is horizontal, so \(z_1 = z_2\). The diameter is constant, so continuity gives \(v_1 = v_2\). Everything cancels except the pressure:

    \[ h_L = \frac{p_1 - p_2}{\gamma} = \frac{4\,\class{k-tw}{\tau_w}\,L}{\gamma D} \]

    The energy friction takes shows up as a pressure drop. This is why the slides use \(h_L = \Delta p/\gamma\) on slide 19.

  4. 4

    Ask what τw depends on

    The wall shear can depend on the fluid (\(\rho\), \(\eta\)), the flow (\(v\)) and the pipe (\(D\), roughness \(\varepsilon\)). Dimensional analysis groups those six quantities into three dimensionless ones:

    \[ \frac{\class{k-tw}{\tau_w}}{\rho v^2} = \phi\!\left(\frac{vD\rho}{\eta},\ \frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right) = \phi\!\left(N_R,\ \frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right) \]

    This is the step where the Reynolds number enters head loss. Wall friction, in dimensionless form, is a function of \(N_R\) and relative roughness only. That's what the Moody diagram plots.

  5. 5

    Define the friction factor

    \[ \class{k-f}{f} \equiv \frac{8\,\class{k-tw}{\tau_w}}{\rho v^2} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \class{k-tw}{\tau_w} = \frac{\class{k-f}{f}}{8}\,\rho v^2 \]

    \(f\) is the wall shear stress in dimensionless form, so \(f = \phi(N_R,\ \varepsilon/D)\) as well. The 8 is a convention. It is chosen so the final formula comes out with \(v^2/2g\) in it.

  6. 6

    Substitute, using γ = ρg

    \[ h_L = \frac{4L}{\rho g D}\cdot\frac{\class{k-f}{f}}{8}\,\rho v^2 \]
    \[ h_L = \class{k-f}{f}\;\class{k-ld}{\frac{L}{D}}\;\class{k-vh}{\frac{v^2}{2g}} \]

    This is the Darcy equation. The density cancels, the 4 and the 8 leave a ½, and \(v^2/(2g)\) appears.

Reading the result

f · friction factor

How hard the wall drags, with no units. It depends only on \(N_R\) and \(\varepsilon/D\). In laminar flow \(f = 64/N_R\). In turbulent flow it comes from the Moody diagram or the Colebrook equation.

L/D · length in diameters

How many diameters long the pipe is. The Fox exercise pipe, 100 m long and 75 mm across, is 1,333 diameters long.

v²/2g · velocity head

The fluid's kinetic energy per unit weight, in metres or feet. Head loss counts how many velocity heads friction takes.

Put the three together for the Fox exercise: \(f\,L/D = 0.0162 \times 1333 = 21.6\). Friction takes 21.6 velocity heads, and each velocity head is \(2.26^2/(2 \times 9.81) = 0.261\) m. So \(h_L = 21.6 \times 0.261 = 5.6\) m.

The same "count of velocity heads" idea returns in Chapter 10. Valves and fittings get a loss \(K\,v^2/2g\), and a straight pipe is the special case \(K = f L/D\).

Slides 18–21

The laminar case: where 64/NR comes from

In laminar flow you can solve for τw exactly, so f needs no chart. This is the derivation on slide 19, written out in full.

  1. 1

    Balance forces on an inner cylinder

    Use the slide's force balance, but on a fluid cylinder of radius \(r\) instead of the whole pipe. Write \(\Delta p = p_1 - p_2\) for the pressure drop.

    \[ \Delta p\,\pi r^2 = -\,\eta\,\frac{dv}{dr}\,2\pi r L \]

    \(dv/dr\) is negative (velocity falls toward the wall), so the minus sign keeps both sides positive. The slide writes \(-\Delta p\) with \(\Delta p = p_2 - p_1\). It is the same equation.

  2. 2

    Integrate and apply v = 0 at the wall

    \[ \frac{dv}{dr} = -\frac{\Delta p\,r}{2\eta L} \;\Rightarrow\; v = -\frac{\Delta p\,r^2}{4\eta L} + C \] \[ v(R) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; v(r) = \frac{\Delta p}{4\eta L}\left(R^2 - r^2\right) \]

    A parabola, fastest on the centreline: \(v_{max} = \Delta p R^2/(4\eta L)\).

  3. 3

    Average over the cross-section

    \[ V = \frac{1}{\pi R^2}\int_0^R v\,(2\pi r\,dr) = \frac{\Delta p\,R^2}{8\eta L} = \frac{v_{max}}{2} \]
  4. 4

    Solve for Δp, then head loss

    \[ \Delta p = \frac{8\eta L V}{R^2} = \frac{32\,\eta L V}{D^2} \;\Rightarrow\; h_L = \frac{\Delta p}{\gamma} = \frac{32\,\eta L V}{\gamma D^2} \]

    This is the Hagen–Poiseuille equation. With \(V = 4Q/(\pi D^2)\) it becomes \(h_L = 128\,\eta L Q/(\pi\gamma D^4)\).

  5. 5

    Rewrite it in Darcy form

    \[ \frac{32\,\eta L V}{\rho g D^2} = \underbrace{\frac{64\,\eta}{\rho V D}}_{\class{k-f}{64/N_R}}\cdot\class{k-ld}{\frac{L}{D}}\cdot\class{k-vh}{\frac{V^2}{2g}} \]
    \[ \class{k-f}{f_{\text{laminar}} = \frac{64}{N_R}} \]

    Check it by multiplying out: \(\frac{64\eta}{\rho V D}\cdot\frac{L}{D}\cdot\frac{V^2}{2g} = \frac{32\eta L V}{\rho g D^2}\). Same result.

Why turbulent flow has no formula like this

In turbulent flow, eddies carry momentum across the pipe. This adds the Reynolds stress on top of viscous stress (slide 24), and nobody can solve for it by hand. The profile also gets flatter in the core and much steeper at the wall, which raises \(\tau_w\). So \(f\) for turbulent flow comes from experiments: the Moody diagram, or the Colebrook equation fitted to it.

How hL scales

The two regimes respond very differently to the same change. This table is the fastest way to answer "what happens if…" questions, like the honey straw on slide 21.

Puzzle

In laminar flow, \(f = 64/N_R\) falls as velocity rises, yet \(h_L\) rises. No contradiction: \(f \propto 1/v\) while \(v^2/2g \propto v^2\), so \(h_L \propto v\).

Reading the chart by hand

  1. Find \(N_R\) on the bottom axis.
  2. Find the relative roughness curve on the right axis. Interpolate between curves if needed.
  3. Follow that curve left to your \(N_R\), then read \(f\) on the left axis. Both axes are logarithmic, so the gridlines are not evenly spaced.

Right of the dashed line, in complete turbulence, every curve is flat. There \(f\) depends only on roughness, which is why slide 24 says \(f\) "remains rather constant when \(N_R\) is very high".

Trap

The two charts in your slides label roughness the opposite way. Slide 25 uses \(\varepsilon/D\) (small numbers like 0.001). Mott's chart on slide 27 uses \(D/\varepsilon\) (large numbers like 1000). Slide 26 says "D = 50e", which means \(D/\varepsilon = 50\), or \(\varepsilon/D = 0.02\).

Trap

Roughness only matters in turbulent flow. For \(N_R < 2000\), use \(f = 64/N_R\) whatever the pipe is made of.

Equations that replace the chart

Colebrook (slide 39) is the equation the Moody curves are drawn from. It is implicit, because \(f\) appears on both sides, so you iterate: guess \(f\), compute the right side, repeat until it stops changing.

\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{f}} = -2.0\,\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon/D}{3.7} + \frac{2.51}{N_R\sqrt{f}}\right) \]

Swamee–Jain is an explicit fit to Colebrook, good to about 1% for \(5000 \le N_R \le 10^8\). It gives \(f\) in one step, which makes it handy on a calculator:

\[ f = \frac{0.25}{\left[\log_{10}\!\left(\dfrac{1}{3.7\,(D/\varepsilon)} + \dfrac{5.74}{N_R^{0.9}}\right)\right]^2} \]

Hazen–Williams (slides 41–43) skips \(f\) entirely: \(v = 0.85\,C_h R^{0.63} s^{0.54}\) in SI, or \(v = 1.32\,C_h R^{0.63} s^{0.54}\) in US units. Here \(R = D/4\) is the hydraulic radius for a full pipe and \(s = h_L/L\). It is empirical, works only for water, and its constants change with the unit system. Typical \(C_h\): PVC 150, copper 130–140, new cast iron 130, concrete 100–140, steel 90–110.

Slide 30 asks what changes once friction is in the picture. Without friction, the pump only lifts the water: \(h_A = \Delta z\). With friction it must also pay for the head loss: \(h_A = \Delta z + h_L\). The slides solve for the pump's gauge pressure as \(p = \gamma\,(\Delta z + h_L)\). The velocity head at the pump is spent when the water slows down entering the reservoir, so it drops out.

Example 8.1 · Glycerin in a 150 mm passageSlide 12 · Is the flow laminar or turbulent?NR = 708, laminar

  2. \(N_R = \dfrac{vD\rho}{\eta} = \dfrac{(3.6)(0.15)(1258)}{0.960} = 708\)

Honey through a strawSlide 21 · Four straws, or one straw twice as wide?Wider straw, 4× better
  1. Honey is so viscous that the flow is laminar, so use Hagen–Poiseuille: \(h_L = \dfrac{128\,\eta L Q}{\pi\gamma D^4}\).

Self-test

Check yourself

Answer each one out loud before you open it. If you can explain all eight, you understand the chapter's two big ideas.

Why does the Reynolds number compare ρv² with ηv/D, and what does each stand for?answer

\(\rho v^2\) is the inertial stress, the momentum flux the fluid carries. \(\eta v/D\) is the viscous stress, viscosity times a typical velocity gradient. Their ratio is \(vD\rho/\eta\). A large ratio means momentum wins and disturbances grow into turbulence. A small ratio means viscosity wins and damps them out.

At which step does NR enter the derivation of the Darcy equation?answer

Step 4. The force balance and energy equation give \(h_L = 4\tau_w L/(\gamma D)\) with no Reynolds number in sight. The Reynolds number appears when you ask what \(\tau_w\) depends on: \(\tau_w/(\rho v^2) = \phi(N_R,\ \varepsilon/D)\). The friction factor is that same group times 8.

Where does the "2g" in v²/2g come from, and why the 8 in f = 8τw/ρv²?answer

The 8 is a choice. Substituting \(\tau_w = (f/8)\rho v^2\) into \(4\tau_w L/(\rho g D)\) gives \((4/8)\,f\,(L/D)\,v^2/g = f\,(L/D)\,v^2/(2g)\). The 8 makes the result come out in velocity heads, \(v^2/2g\), which match the energy equation's kinetic-energy term.

In laminar flow, the velocity doubles. What happens to f and to hL?answer

\(N_R\) doubles, so \(f = 64/N_R\) halves. But \(v^2/2g\) goes up 4 times. Net: \(h_L\) doubles. In fully rough turbulent flow, \(f\) stays put and \(h_L\) goes up 4 times.

The laminar pressure drop Δp = 32ηLV/D² has no density in it. Why?answer

In fully developed laminar flow nothing accelerates. Each layer slides at a constant speed, so inertia never comes into play, and density only matters through inertia. Viscosity alone sets the resistance. Head loss \(h_L = \Delta p/\gamma\) does depend on density, but only because converting pressure to head divides by \(\gamma = \rho g\).

Why is the average velocity exactly half of vmax in laminar flow?answer

The profile is a paraboloid, \(v = v_{max}(1 - r^2/R^2)\). Averaging over the circular area weights each ring by \(2\pi r\,dr\), and \(\int_0^R (1 - r^2/R^2)\,2r\,dr / R^2 = 1/2\). The slow fluid near the wall covers a lot of area, which pulls the average down. Turbulent profiles are flatter, so their average is about 0.8 of the maximum.

You get NR = 3,000. What do you do?answer

You are in the critical zone, where the flow may be laminar or turbulent and \(f\) can't be predicted. Say so, and if it is a design problem, change the diameter or flow rate to get out of the zone. If you must estimate, compute both \(64/N_R\) and the turbulent value and treat the result as a range.

Variation 1 swaps smooth pipe for concrete. Which of v, NR, f and p change?answer

Only \(f\), and with it \(h_L\) and \(p\). Velocity comes from \(Q/A\), and \(N_R\) from velocity, diameter and the fluid. Roughness doesn't affect either one. It only moves you to a different curve on the Moody diagram.

Wall shear and f\[ \Delta p = \frac{4\tau_w L}{D},\quad f = \frac{8\tau_w}{\rho v^2} \]The two facts the derivation rests on.
Colebrook\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt f} = -2\log\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon/D}{3.7} + \frac{2.51}{N_R\sqrt f}\right) \]Turbulent. Iterate.
Swamee–Jain\[ f = \frac{0.25}{\left[\log\!\left(\frac{1}{3.7(D/\varepsilon)} + \frac{5.74}{N_R^{0.9}}\right)\right]^2} \]Turbulent, explicit, about 1% from Colebrook.
Energy equation\[ \frac{p_1}{\gamma} + z_1 + \frac{v_1^2}{2g} + h_A - h_L = \frac{p_2}{\gamma} + z_2 + \frac{v_2^2}{2g} \]Chapter 7, now with hL from Darcy.
Pump power\[ P_A = h_A\,\gamma\,Q, \qquad P_I = \frac{P_A}{e_M} \]Power added to the fluid, then power input.
