The General Energy Equation

Bernoulli assumed nothing adds or removes energy between two points. Real pipelines have pumps that add it, turbines and motors that take it out, and friction that wastes it. Three new heads, \(h_A\), \(h_R\) and \(h_L\), keep the books balanced.

Slides 6–9, 20–21 · Mott §7.3–7.4

The general energy equation

Keep Bernoulli's energy-per-unit-weight bookkeeping and add three terms, each also in feet or metres of head:

  • \(h_A\): energy added by a pump.
  • \(h_R\): energy removed for useful work by a turbine or fluid motor.
  • \(h_L\): energy lost to friction in pipes, valves and fittings (it becomes heat).
\[ \frac{p_1}{\gamma} + z_1 + \frac{v_1^2}{2g} + h_A - h_R - h_L = \frac{p_2}{\gamma} + z_2 + \frac{v_2^2}{2g} \]

Read it as: energy at 1, plus what's added, minus what's taken out, equals energy at 2. Flow goes from 1 to 2. Like Bernoulli, it can be written per unit volume (multiply through by γ), which the slides skip for time.

Slide 21: use it either way. Given the pump head and losses, find a pressure, velocity or elevation; or given the pressures you need, find the pump head.

Reading Mott's Figure 7.3 (slide 20)

Between point 1 (before the pump) and point 2 (after the motor), the fluid gains \(h_A\) in the pump, loses \(h_L\) in the pipe, valve and elbows, and gives up \(h_R\) in the motor. The energy at 2 is the energy at 1 plus \(h_A - h_R - h_L\).

Pumps (slide 13) and motors (slide 19)

Positive-displacement pumps trap a fixed volume and push it out (gear, piston, vane). Kinetic pumps (centrifugal) add speed with an impeller and convert it to pressure. A hydraulic motor is a pump run backward: pressure in, shaft work out. Chapter 13 covers pumps in detail.

Trap

Units: \(h_A\), \(h_R\), \(h_L\) are N·m/N = m (or lb·ft/lb = ft). They are energies per unit weight, not powers and not pressures.

Slides 15–18 · Mott §7.6–7.7

Power and efficiency

Head is energy per pound (or newton) of fluid; the weight flow rate \(W = \gamma Q\) is pounds per second. Multiply:

\[ P_A = h_A W = h_A\gamma Q \]

The pump's shaft must supply more than that, because of friction and leakage inside the pump. The mechanical efficiency is

\[ e_M = \frac{P_A}{P_I} < 1 \]

For a fluid motor it runs the other way: the fluid delivers \(P_R = h_R\gamma Q\) and the shaft gets \(P_O = e_M P_R\).

Conversions: 1 hp = 550 ft·lb/s = 745.7 W; 1 ft·lb/s = 1.356 W.

Slides 22–27

A pumped system, one step at a time

Water is pumped from an open reservoir to another open reservoir 6 ft higher. The slides add complications one at a time; the energy-line diagram at the top of the page follows along.

  1. 1

    No losses (slide 23)

    Surface to surface: \(p = 0\) and \(v \approx 0\) at both ends, so \(z_1 + h_A = z_2\), \(h_A = 6\) ft. Even a frictionless pump must lift the water.

  2. 2

    9 ft of loss in the discharge line (slide 24)

    \(h_A = 6 + 9 = 15\) ft. At 2 ft³/s and 80 % efficiency: \(P_I = 15(62.4)(2)/0.8 = 2340\) ft·lb/s = 4.25 hp.

  3. 3

    Pressure at the pump outlet (slide 25)

    From the pump outlet (10 ft below the upper surface, 10 ft/s) to the upper surface: \(\dfrac{p_o}{\gamma} + \dfrac{v^2}{2g} - 9 = 10\), so \(\dfrac{p_o}{\gamma} = 10 + 9 - \dfrac{100}{64.4} = 17.4\) ft.

  4. 4

    Add 5 ft of loss in the suction line (slide 26)

    \(h_A = 6 + 9 + 5 = 20\) ft. The pump makes up every loss plus the lift.

  5. 5

    Pressure at the pump inlet (slide 27)

    From the lower surface to the pump inlet (4 ft below it): \(4 - 5 = \dfrac{p_i}{\gamma} + \dfrac{v^2}{2g}\), so \(\dfrac{p_i}{\gamma} = 4 - 5 - 1.55 = -2.55\) ft: below atmospheric. At 20 ft/s the velocity head is 6.21 ft and the inlet drops to −7.21 ft.

    Slide 28: the low inlet pressure comes partly from the suction-line loss and partly from converting pressure head into velocity head.

Slides 28–29 · more in Mott §13.11 (NPSH)

Too low a pressure: flashing and cavitation

Water boils when its pressure falls to its vapor pressure, at any temperature. At room temperature that is about 2.3 kPa absolute (roughly 0.8 ft of water at 70 °F), far below atmospheric. If the pressure at a pump inlet falls that low, vapor bubbles form (flashing). Carried into the higher-pressure part of the pump they collapse violently (cavitation), which is noisy, cuts the flow and pits the impeller.

So compare absolute pressure heads: atmospheric is about 33.9 ft (10.3 m) of water. In slide 27's case at 20 ft/s the inlet sits at 33.9 − 7.2 ≈ 26.7 ft absolute, still safe. Push the velocity or suction loss far enough in the diagram at the top and it warns you. Designers keep suction lines short, wide and with few fittings, and put pumps below the supply surface when they can. Chapter 13 turns this into the net positive suction head (NPSH) check.

Pipe sizes and schedules

A "4-inch" pipe isn't 4 inches across anywhere. The nominal size names a family with a fixed outside diameter; the schedule sets the wall thickness, so a heavier schedule has a smaller inside diameter. Flow calculations need the inside diameter.

s14–18Pump to make up a pipeline's losshL = 2 ft, Q = 5 ft³/s of water; then 50 % efficient1.13 hp; 2.27 hp
  1. \(h_A = h_L = 2\) ft (same pressure, velocity and elevation at both ends).
  2. \(P_A = h_A\gamma Q = 2(62.4)(5) = 624\) ft·lb/s ÷ 550 = 1.13 hp.
  3. \(P_I = P_A/e_M = 1.13/0.5 = 2.27\) hp.
s23Lift with no lossesReservoir to reservoir, 6 ft uphA = 6 ft

Both surfaces are open (p = 0) and still (v = 0), so only elevation is left: \(h_A = z_2 - z_1 = 6\) ft.

s24Group discussion · lift plus loss6 ft lift, 9 ft discharge loss, 2 ft³/s, 80 % efficient15 ft; 2340 ft·lb/s = 4.25 hp
  1. \(h_A = 6 + 9 = 15\) ft.
  2. \(P_I = h_A\gamma Q/e_M = 15(62.4)(2)/0.8 = 2340\) ft·lb/s = 4.25 hp. The slide writes this as 30 × 62.4/0.8.
s25Pressure at the pump outlet10 ft/s; outlet 10 ft below the upper surface17.4 ft

Energy equation from the pump outlet to the upper surface: \(\dfrac{p_o}{\gamma} + z_o + \dfrac{v^2}{2g} - 9 = z_U\), with \(z_U - z_o = 10\). So \(\dfrac{p_o}{\gamma} = 9 - \dfrac{100}{2(32.2)} + 10 = 17.4\) ft, matching the slide's expression. That's \(17.4 \times 62.4/144 = 7.6\) psig.

s26Add suction-line loss6 ft lift, 9 ft discharge loss, 5 ft suction loss20 ft

\(h_A = (z_2 - z_1) + h_{L,total} = 6 + 9 + 5 = 20\) ft. Where the loss happens doesn't matter for \(h_A\); it does matter for the pressures along the way.

s27Pressure at the pump inletInlet 4 ft below the lower surface; 10 ft/s, then 20 ft/s−2.55 ft; −7.21 ft
  1. Lower surface to pump inlet: \(z_L - 5 = \dfrac{p_i}{\gamma} + z_i + \dfrac{v^2}{2g}\), with \(z_L - z_i = 4\).
  2. \(\dfrac{p_i}{\gamma} = -5 - \dfrac{100}{64.4} + 4 = -2.55\) ft (−1.1 psig).
  3. At 20 ft/s the velocity head quadruples to 6.21 ft: \(\dfrac{p_i}{\gamma} = -7.21\) ft (−3.1 psig).

Textbook problems, solved

Ex 7.1Total loss from a draining reservoir1.20 ft³/s through a pipe ending in a 3 in free jet, 25 ft below the reservoir surface (12 ft + 13 ft)15.7 ft
  1. Surface (p = 0, v = 0) to free jet (p = 0): \(z_1 - h_L = z_2 + v_2^2/2g\), so \(h_L = (z_1 - z_2) - v_2^2/2g\).
  2. \(v_2 = 1.20/0.04909 = 24.4\) ft/s, \(v_2^2/2g = 9.28\) ft.
  3. \(h_L = 25 - 9.28 = 15.72\) ft, as on the slide. Mott's text rounds the area to 0.0491 ft² and gets 15.75 ft.

More than half the available 25 ft is lost to the entrance, valve, elbows and pipe friction.

Ex 7.3Pump efficiency from a manometerOil 56.0 lb/ft³ at 500 gal/min; 6 in Sch 40 in, 4 in Sch 40 out; mercury manometer reads 20.4 in; input 3.85 hpeM ≈ 0.77
  1. Manometer (the unknown leg height cancels): \(\dfrac{p_2 - p_1}{\gamma_o} = \left(\dfrac{\gamma_m}{\gamma_o} - 1\right)(20.4\text{ in}) = \left(\dfrac{844.9}{56.0} - 1\right)(1.70\text{ ft}) = 24.0\) ft.
  2. \(Q = 500/449 = 1.114\) ft³/s. Areas 0.2006 and 0.0884 ft², so \(v_1 = 5.55\), \(v_2 = 12.6\) ft/s and \((v_2^2 - v_1^2)/2g = 1.99\) ft.
  3. \(h_A = 24.0 + 0 + 1.99 = 26.0\) ft; \(P_A = 26.0(56.0)(1.114)/550 = 2.95\) hp.
  4. \(e_M = 2.95/3.85 = 0.77\). Carrying full precision gives 0.76; the book rounds the manometer ratio to 15.1.
7.11Deep-well pump745 gal/h through 1 in Sch 40 to a tank at 40 psig, 120 ft above the well level; 10.5 ft of loss; pump draws 1 hp0.700 hp; 70 %
  1. Well surface (p = 0, v = 0) to the tank's water surface (p = 40 psig, v = 0): \(h_A = 120 + \dfrac{40(144)}{62.4} + 10.5 = 120 + 92.3 + 10.5 = 222.8\) ft.
  2. \(Q = 745/(449 \times 60) = 0.02765\) ft³/s.
  3. \(P_A = 222.8(62.4)(0.02765) = 384\) ft·lb/s = 0.700 hp. Matches the book.
  4. \(e_M = 0.700/1 = 70\) %.

The 1 in pipe size isn't needed because the loss is given. I read the 120 ft as measured from the well level to the tank's water surface, per Mott's figure.

Self-test

Check yourself

1. A pump moves water between two open reservoirs with the same surface level. With 12 ft of total pipe loss, hA is

Surface to surface: p, v and z cancel; h_A = h_L. Velocity heads matter only at points inside pipes.

2. Doubling the flow rate through a pump at the same head multiplies the power by

P_A = h_A γ Q. (In a real system, more flow also means more loss, so the head rises too; Chapter 11.)

3. Which change makes cavitation at a pump inlet more likely?

Hot water has a higher vapor pressure, so it boils at a higher pressure. The other three all raise the inlet pressure.
