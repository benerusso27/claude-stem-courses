Drag and Lift

A body moving through a fluid is pushed backward (drag) and, if it's shaped or tilted right, sideways (lift). Both scale the same way: a coefficient times the dynamic pressure \(\rho V^2/2\) times an area. Shape sets the coefficient, and the boundary layer, whether it stays attached, sets the shape's effect.

Slide 2 · Mott §17.5

Low vs high Reynolds number

Stokes flow (NR ≪ 1)

Bacteria, fine dust, the coins sinking in honey from the lab. Viscosity dominates and drag is proportional to V¹. For a sphere, Stokes' law gives \(F_D = 3\pi\eta Vd\), which is the same as \(C_D = 24/N_R\). The course skips this regime except here.

High NR (most engineering)

Cars, balls, buildings, wings. Drag is proportional to V², and the useful idea is the boundary layer: the thin layer near the surface where the flow is slowed by viscosity.

Slides 3–6, 13–14 · Mott §17.3–17.4

Boundary layers, separation, and two kinds of drag

  • Skin (friction) drag: shear stress in the boundary layer, summed over the wetted surface. It grows with surface area, and with a turbulent boundary layer it too scales with V² (slide 6).
  • Form (pressure) drag: if the boundary layer can't follow the surface around the back, it separates, leaving a low-pressure wake. High pressure in front, low behind: a net backward push.

Why a turbulent boundary layer resists separation (slide 13): turbulent mixing keeps feeding fast outer fluid down to the wall. That near-wall fluid has more momentum to push against the rising pressure at the back of a body, so it stays attached longer and the wake is narrower.

Roughness (slide 14): dimples on a golf ball trip the boundary layer to turbulent at a lower speed. Friction drag rises a little, but the wake shrinks so much that total drag drops sharply. Mott says a perfectly smooth golf ball could be driven only about 100 yards.

Slides 7–8 · Mott §17.2, Table 17.1

The drag coefficient

\[ F_D = C_D\,\frac{\rho V^2}{2}\,A \]
  • \(C_D\) depends heavily on shape and weakly on NR (except for rounded bodies, where the separation point moves: next section).
  • A is the frontal (projected) area for blunt bodies, but the planform area (seen from above) for wings (slide 7).
  • Typical values (slide 8): cars about 0.25 at best, trucks up to 0.75, aircraft 0.1–0.2.

Streamlining trades pressure drag for friction drag: a long tapered tail keeps the flow attached but adds wetted area. At moderate to high NR, pressure drag usually dominates, so streamlining pays. Compare the streamlined body (0.04) with the sphere (0.47) in the chart at the top: the same frontal area, a twelfth of the drag.

Slides 10–12 · Mott §17.4, Fig. 17.4

Spheres: the drag crisis and terminal velocity

For a smooth sphere, \(C_D\) falls steeply through the Stokes range, levels off near 0.4–0.5, then drops suddenly to about 0.2 around \(N_R \approx 2\)–\(3\times10^5\) when the boundary layer turns turbulent (slide 10). A rough sphere makes the drop earlier and shallower.

Terminal velocity (slide 12): falling at constant speed, weight = buoyancy + drag:

\[ (\rho_s - \rho_f)\,g\,\frac{\pi d^3}{6} = C_D\,\frac{\rho_f V^2}{2}\,\frac{\pi d^2}{4} \]

But \(C_D\) depends on \(N_R\), which depends on V. So: guess \(C_D\), solve for V, compute \(N_R\), read a new \(C_D\), repeat. The calculator does that loop with an approximate smooth-sphere curve.

The sphere curve here is a rough log-log interpolation through typical values with the shape of Mott Fig. 17.4; near the drag crisis it can be off by 20 % or more. Use the textbook chart on graded work.

Slides 15–21 · Mott §17.8–17.9

Lift on an airfoil

\[ F_L = C_L\,\frac{\rho V^2}{2}\,A,\qquad F_D = C_D\,\frac{\rho V^2}{2}\,A \]

with A the planform area, chord × span (slide 18). Why does a wing lift? Slide 16's point: "it's not that simple." The wing turns the passing air downward (the Coanda effect helps the flow follow the upper surface), so by momentum the air pushes the wing up; equivalently, the flow over the top is faster and at lower pressure. A crude turning model (slide 17) gives \(F_L \propto \sin\alpha\) and \(F_D \propto 1 - \cos\alpha\): not precise, but the right trend.

  • CL rises almost linearly with angle of attack α until the stall, where the flow separates from the upper surface and lift collapses.
  • Cruise: low α, best \(F_L/F_D\). Takeoff and landing: high α (and flaps) for maximum CL at low speed.

Slide examples, solved

s9Mobile home overturned by wind (Mott 17.12)50 kN; body 2.5 m tall on a 0.5 m chassis; tires 2 m apart, each 300 mm wide; treat as a square cylinder; air at 0 °C≈ 31 m/s (10 m long)

Assumptions: drag acts at mid-height of the body, 0.5 + 1.25 = 1.75 m above the ground; the home tips about the outer edge of the downwind tire, 1.0 + 0.15 = 1.15 m from the centerline (tire centers 2 m apart); CD ≈ 2.05, the high-NR end of Mott's square-cylinder curve (Fig. 17.4b); ρ = 1.292 kg/m³ at 0 °C.

  1. Tipping when \(F_D(1.75) = (50\,000)(1.15)\): \(F_D = 32.9\) kN.
  2. \(F_D = C_D\tfrac12\rho V^2 A\) with \(A = L \times 2.5\) m. For Mott's L = 10 m: \(V^2 = 32\,900/(2.05 \times 0.646 \times 25) = 992\), V ≈ 31 m/s (113 km/h).

Slide 9 gives the length as 15 m. With 15 m the side area is 37.5 m² and V drops to about 26 m/s. The slide's ~33 m/s is close to the 10 m result (the small difference comes from the tipping point, CD and density chosen), so the 15 m looks like a slip. Check which length your instructor means.

s11Weather ball (Mott 17.2)2 m sphere in 0 °C air; winds 15 to 160 km/h≈ 10 N at 5 m/s
  1. At 15 km/h (4.2 m/s) to 18 km/h (5 m/s): \(N_R = Vd/\nu \approx (5)(2)/1.5\times10^{-5} \approx 7\times10^5\), past the drag crisis, so \(C_D \approx 0.2\) (slide).
  2. \(F_D = 0.2 \times \tfrac12(1.29)(5)^2 \times \pi(1)^2 = 10\) N.
  3. For the higher speeds \(N_R\) grows past 10⁶; CD stays near 0.2 on Mott's chart and the force grows with V²: roughly 4× per doubling of speed.

At 15 km/h exactly, NR ≈ 5.6 × 10⁵ with ν = 1.5 × 10⁻⁵ m²/s (Mott lists ν ≈ 1.33 × 10⁻⁵ at 0 °C, which gives 6.3 × 10⁵); either way it's just past the crisis, which is where reading CD is least certain.

s12Terminal velocity of an aluminum sphere (Mott 17.3c)75 mm, 26.6 kN/m³, falling in air at 20 °C≈ 105 m/s
  1. Net weight: \((26\,600 - 11.8)\,\pi(0.075)^3/6 = 5.87\) N (buoyancy in air is negligible).
  2. Guess \(C_D = 0.1\): \(V = \sqrt{5.87/(0.1 \times 0.602 \times 4.42\times10^{-3})} = 149\) m/s, \(N_R \approx 7.4\times10^5\).
  3. At that NR the chart gives \(C_D \approx 0.2\): V = 105 m/s, \(N_R \approx 5.2\times10^5\), still about 0.2. Converged (slide). The calculator's rough curve gives 109 m/s.
  4. Parts (a) and (b), same sphere: in water, CD ≈ 0.45 and V ≈ 1.9 m/s; in castor oil (η = 0.651 Pa·s), \(N_R \approx 150\) and V ≈ 1.4 m/s. Try them in the calculator.
s19Exercise 17.28 · inverted wing on a race carα = 15°, 25 m/s, chord 780 mm, span 1460 mm, ρ = 1.2 kg/m³534 N down; 43 N drag
  1. \(A = 0.78 \times 1.46 = 1.139\) m², \(\tfrac12\rho V^2 = 375\) Pa.
  2. From the chart at 15°: \(C_L = 1.25\), so \(F_L = 1.25 \times 375 \times 1.139 = 534\) N, pushing down (the wing is upside down).
  3. \(C_D = 0.10\): \(F_D = 43\) N. The book asks for the drag too.
s20Exercise 17.32 · heaviest plane that can take offα = 15°, 125 km/h, chord 1.4 m, span 6.8 m8.6 kN; 689 N drag
  1. V = 34.7 m/s, \(\tfrac12\rho V^2 = 723\) Pa, A = 9.52 m².
  2. \(F_L = 1.25 \times 723 \times 9.52 = 8.61\) kN: the weight the wing can lift.
  3. \(F_D = 0.10 \times 723 \times 9.52 = 689\) N.
s21Exercise 17.32 extended · cruising at 250 km/hSame plane and weightα ≈ 2°; drag ≈ 300 N
  1. Lift must still equal 8.61 kN, but \(\tfrac12\rho V^2\) is 4× larger (2,894 Pa), so \(C_L = 8610/(2894 \times 9.52) = 0.31\).
  2. On the chart, \(C_L = 0.31\) at about 2°. There \(C_D \approx 0.011\), so \(F_D \approx 0.011 \times 2894 \times 9.52 \approx 300\) N: less drag at twice the speed, because the wing flies at a much more efficient angle.
