Pressure in Static Fluids

A fluid at rest can't resist shear, so the only stress it can carry is pressure. Pressure grows linearly with depth, \(\Delta p = \gamma h\), and it doesn't care about the shape of the container. That one rule runs every manometer and barometer in this chapter.

Slides 2–4

Two pop questions to start

Answer before you open the explanation. They set up everything else in the chapter.

Pop question 1 (slide 2). "Nothing moves in a static fluid" and "a fluid under shear stress deforms indefinitely." Are these contradictory?

No. Put them together and they say: a static fluid must have no shear stress anywhere. If there were any, the fluid would be moving.

Pop question 2 (slide 3). Does that mean there is no stress at all in a static fluid?

No. A static fluid can carry normal stress, pressure, as long as it pushes equally from every direction on a tiny fluid element. Equal pushes from all sides squeeze the element without changing its shape, so it doesn't flow (slide 4).

Slide 4's last line is the key to the chapter: "often the difference in pressure is what matters." The absolute level of pressure squeezes everything equally and moves nothing. A pressure that changes from place to place produces a net force, and in a static fluid that net force must be balanced by gravity.

That balance gives \(\Delta p = \gamma h\), derived next. It also explains why gauge pressure is so useful (slides 5–6): when the atmosphere pushes on both sides of a wall, only the gauge pressure produces a net force.

Slides 7–10 · Mott §3.3–3.4

The hydrostatic equation

How much extra pressure do you feel at depth h? Balance the forces on a column of fluid and the answer falls out.

  1. 1

    Isolate a vertical column

    Take a column of liquid with cross-section \(A\), from the surface (pressure \(p_0\)) down to depth \(h\) (pressure \(p\)). The fluid is at rest, so the vertical forces on the column must balance.

  2. 2

    List the forces

    Down: the pressure on top, \(p_0 A\), and the column's weight, \(W = \gamma V = \gamma A h\). Up: the pressure on the bottom, \(pA\). The side pressures are horizontal and cancel each other.

    \[ pA = p_0 A + \gamma A h \]
  3. 3

    Divide by A

    \[ p - p_0 = \gamma h = \rho g h \]

    \(A\) cancels, so the result doesn't depend on the width of the column, or on the shape of the container. If the surface is open to the air, \(p_0 = 0\) gauge and \(p = \gamma h\) is a gauge pressure (slide 7's question).

Which way is h? (slide 8)

\(h\) is measured downward from the point where you know the pressure. If your problem uses an elevation \(z\) measured upward, then \(h = -\Delta z\) and the equation reads \(\Delta p = -\gamma\,\Delta z\): pressure falls as you go up. Pick a reference, write it on your sketch, and keep it for the whole problem.

When does it apply? (slide 10)

  • Fluid at rest, constant density. Liquids, always. Gases over modest heights only: across a room the air's pressure change is tiny, but across kilometres of atmosphere density changes, and that is beyond this course.
  • Any connected path. You can go from one point to another through any route that stays in the same continuous fluid, however tortuous. Only the vertical distance matters. This is what makes manometers work.
  • Pascal's paradox (Mott §3.5): the vessels above hold very different weights of liquid, yet the pressure at a given depth is the same in all of them, because the walls carry the extra weight.

Slide 11 · Mott §3.7

The barometer

A tube closed at the top, filled with liquid, and turned upside down in a dish. The liquid falls until the column's weight balances the atmosphere.

Above the liquid in the tube is (nearly) a vacuum, so the pressure there is zero absolute. Walk down the column to the dish surface, where the atmosphere acts:

\[ p_{atm} = 0 + \gamma h \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad h = \frac{p_{atm}}{\gamma} \]

A barometer measures absolute pressure, because its reference is a vacuum, not the atmosphere. Mercury is used because it is so dense (sg 13.54) that the column is a manageable 760 mm. With water it would be over 10 m tall, and water also evaporates into the "vacuum" at the top.

Slides 13–20 · Mott §3.6

Manometers: walk the path

A manometer is a bent tube of liquid. Read the levels, then walk from a point of known pressure to the unknown one, adding γh going down and subtracting going up.

Mott's procedure (§3.6), condensed:

  1. Start where the pressure is known (an open end is \(p_{atm}\) = 0 gauge). Write it down.
  2. Move through the fluid. Going down a height \(h\) in a fluid of specific weight \(\gamma\), add \(\gamma h\). Going up, subtract it.
  3. Jump horizontally freely within one continuous fluid: points at the same level have the same pressure. Don't jump across an interface between two different fluids.
  4. Stop at the point you want. The expression you have built equals that pressure.

The inclined manometer (slide 20)

Tilt the reading tube at angle \(\theta\) from horizontal. The liquid still rises only the vertical height \(h\) the pressure difference requires, but it travels a length \(L = h/\sin\theta\) along the tube. At \(\theta = 30°\), \(\sin\theta = 0.5\), so every millimetre of vertical rise spreads over 2 mm of scale: twice the resolution. That is why inclined manometers are used for small pressure differences, like air in ducts.

Use the vertical height in \(\gamma h\), never the length along the tube.

Slide 21 · Mott §3.9

Diaphragm and Bourdon gauges

The dial gauges you see on tanks and pipes work mechanically. In a diaphragm gauge, pressure flexes a thin metal membrane, and a linkage turns that small motion into a pointer rotation. A Bourdon tube is a curved, flattened tube that straightens slightly under pressure. Both have the atmosphere on their outside, so they read gauge pressure. They are convenient and rugged, but less accurate than a manometer and they need calibration.

Self-test

Check yourself

1. A diver goes from 2 m to 12 m deep in fresh water. Her gauge pressure rises by about

Δp = γΔh = 9.81 × 10 = 98.1 kPa. The change depends only on the 10 m difference.

2. A dam holds a lake 30 m deep. A second dam holds a narrow canal of the same depth. The pressure at the base of the dam is

Pressure depends only on depth: γh = 294 kPa at both. The lake's size doesn't matter (Pascal's paradox).

3. In a manometer, you move from point 3 to point 4, which is 0.4 m higher, in water. You should

Going up, pressure decreases: subtract γ_w(0.4) = 3.92 kPa.

4. A barometer is moved from sea level to a mountain. The mercury column

The atmosphere is lower on the mountain, and h = p_atm/γ. A barometer reads absolute pressure.

Why can you jump horizontally between two points in the same fluid, but not across a fluid interface?answer

Within one continuous fluid at rest, Δp = γΔh, so equal heights mean equal pressures, whatever path connects them. At a horizontal line that crosses from, say, water into mercury, the two points are at the same height but reached through different fluids above them, so the columns above weigh different amounts and the pressures differ.

Derive Δp = γh without looking. What assumptions did you make?answer

Force balance on a vertical column: \(pA = p_0A + \gamma Ah\). Assumptions: the fluid is at rest (no acceleration, no shear), γ is constant over the height (incompressible), and gravity is the only body force.
