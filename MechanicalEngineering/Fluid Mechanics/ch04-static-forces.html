Forces on Submerged Surfaces

Pressure grows with depth, so a wall or gate feels more push at the bottom than at the top. Two numbers capture all of it: the size of the resultant force, \(F_R = \gamma h_c A\), and where it acts, the center of pressure, always a little below the centroid.

One method, seven cases

The slides build up from the easy cases to the general one. Every case answers the same three questions: how big is the force, where does it act, and which way does it point.

Direction is the easy one. Pressure always pushes perpendicular to a surface, so the resultant on a flat surface is perpendicular to that surface. Size and location take the rest of the chapter.

Slide 8: why hydraulics multiply force

When the pressure is high, a few metres of depth hardly change it, so the pressure is the same everywhere in the fluid. Force is pressure times area, so a big piston fed from the same fluid as a small one gets a force bigger by the ratio of the areas. An excavator's cylinders run at around 20–35 MPa (typical industry figures, not from the slides), so a 100 mm bore cylinder pushes with roughly 160–270 kN.

The catch: the large piston moves 100 times less far. Energy is conserved.

Slides 13–16 · Mott §4.6

Where FR = γhcA and Lp come from

Slides 15 and 16 ask you to finish the derivation. Here it is, for a plane surface at any angle.

  1. 1

    Add up the pressure on every strip

    A strip at distance \(L\) is at depth \(h = L\cos\theta\), so the force on it is \(dF = p\,dA = \gamma L\cos\theta\,dA\). Integrate over the surface:

    \[ F_R = \int_A \gamma L\cos\theta\,dA = \gamma\cos\theta\int_A L\,dA \]
  2. 2

    Recognize the first moment of area

    By definition of the centroid, \(\int_A L\,dA = L_c A\). So

    \[ F_R = \gamma\,(L_c\cos\theta)\,A = \gamma\,h_c\,A = p_c\,A \]

    The resultant equals the pressure at the centroid times the area. Slide 13 calls this \(P_{avg}\): the average pressure over the surface is the pressure at its centroid.

  3. 3

    Find where it acts: balance moments

    The resultant must produce the same moment about the surface line as all the strips together (slide 16):

    \[ F_R\,L_p = \int_A L\,dF = \gamma\cos\theta\int_A L^2\,dA = \gamma\cos\theta\,I_s \]

    \(I_s\) is the second moment of area about the surface line. The parallel-axis theorem moves it to the centroid: \(I_s = I_c + A L_c^2\).

  4. 4

    Divide by FR

    \[ L_p = \frac{\gamma\cos\theta\,(I_c + AL_c^2)}{\gamma\cos\theta\,L_cA} \]
    \[ L_p = L_c + \frac{I_c}{L_c A} \]

    The extra term is always positive, so the center of pressure is always below the centroid. It shrinks as the gate goes deeper (\(L_c\) grows), because the pressure becomes more uniform across the gate.

Trap

\(L_c\) and \(L_p\) are measured from the free surface line along the plane (slide 13: "always measure from the water surface"), not from the top of the gate.

Trap

\(I_c\) is about the centroidal axis that is horizontal (parallel to the surface line). For a rectangle of width b and height h along the slope, \(I_c = bh^3/12\), with h the dimension along the slope.

Trap

Force uses the vertical depth \(h_c\); location uses distances along the plane, \(L\). On a vertical wall the two are the same, which is why mixing them up only shows on inclined gates.

Slides 18–20 · 2018 Midterm I, Problem 3

Will the dam slide or tip over?

A rectangular earth dam of height H and width W holds water of depth D. Per metre of dam length, compare the water's push with what the dam's weight can resist.

  1. 1

    Water force and its moment

    Vertical wall, top at the surface: \(F_w = \gamma_w (D/2)(D\cdot 1) = \gamma_w D^2/2\), acting \(D/3\) above the base. About the downstream toe A: \(M_w = \gamma_w D^3/6\).

  2. 2

    Sliding

    Friction can supply \(\mu\,\gamma_e H W\). Require \(\mu\gamma_e H W \ge \gamma_w D^2/2\). With \(\mu = 0.5\):

    \[ W \ge \frac{\gamma_w D^2}{\gamma_e H} \]
  3. 3

    Overturning about A

    The dam's weight acts at \(W/2\) from A: \(M_r = \gamma_e H W \cdot W/2\). Require \(M_r \ge M_w\):

    \[ W \ge \sqrt{\frac{\gamma_w D^3}{3\gamma_e H}} \]

    Both match the slide's answers. Real dams also check uplift: water seeping under the base pushes up and reduces both friction and the restoring moment.

Slides 34–37 · Mott §4.8–4.11, Example 4.8

Curved surfaces

Integrating pressure around a curve is messy. Instead, split the force into horizontal and vertical parts and get each from something you already know.

  • Horizontal component \(F_H\): the force on the curve's vertical projection, a flat rectangle of height \(s\) (slide 34: "it may sound like word play, but it is true"). So \(F_H = \gamma s w\,h_c\) with \(h_c = h_1 + s/2\), acting at \(h_p = h_c + s^2/(12h_c)\).
  • Vertical component \(F_V\): the weight of the fluid above the curve, up to the free surface. It acts through the centroid of that fluid volume. Slide 34 says finding that point needs calculus; Mott gets it with the composite-area method and Appendix L, which is what the tool below does.
  • Resultant: \(F_R = \sqrt{F_H^2 + F_V^2}\) at \(\phi = \tan^{-1}(F_V/F_H)\) above horizontal. Every bit of pressure on a circular surface points along a radius, so \(F_R\) passes through the center of curvature. That is a free check on your answer.
  • Fluid below the curve (Mott §4.10): imagine the fluid extending up to the free surface anyway. The magnitudes are the same; the directions flip.

Slide examples, solved

s29–33Q1 · 1 m × 1 m vertical gate, top at the surfaceWater, g = 10 m/s²5.0 kN at 0.667 m
  1. \(h_c = L_c = 0.5\) m, \(A = 1\) m², \(\gamma = 1000 \times 10 = 10{,}000\) N/m³.
  2. \(F_R = \gamma h_c A = (10{,}000)(0.5)(1) = 5000\) N.
  3. \(I_c = bh^3/12 = 1/12\) m⁴, so \(L_p = 0.5 + \dfrac{1/12}{(0.5)(1)} = 0.667\) m: two-thirds of the way down, the classic \(h/3\) from the bottom.
s29–33Q2 · 2 m × 1 m gate tilted 60° from verticalTop edge at the surface; 2 m along the slope, 1 m wide10 kN at Lp = 1.33 m
  1. \(L_c = 1.0\) m along the slope. Vertical depth \(h_c = L_c\cos 60° = 0.5\) m.
  2. \(F_R = (10{,}000)(0.5)(2) = 10{,}000\) N. Twice Q1's area at the same centroid depth: twice the force.
  3. \(I_c = (1)(2)^3/12 = 0.667\) m⁴, so \(L_p = 1.0 + \dfrac{0.667}{(1.0)(2)} = 1.333\) m along the slope (depth 0.667 m).

"Tilted by 60 degrees (so rather horizontal)" means 60° away from vertical, which is 30° above horizontal. That is the reading used here and in the solver.

s29–33Q3 · Same gate, 1 m lowerTop edge now 1 m below the surface (vertically)30 kN at Lp = 3.11 m
  1. The top edge is 1 m deep, so along the slope it is \(1/\cos 60° = 2.0\) m from the surface line. \(L_c = 2.0 + 1.0 = 3.0\) m, \(h_c = 3.0\cos 60° = 1.5\) m.
  2. \(F_R = (10{,}000)(1.5)(2) = 30{,}000\) N.
  3. \(L_p = 3.0 + \dfrac{0.667}{(3.0)(2)} = 3.111\) m. The offset \(L_p - L_c\) dropped from 0.333 to 0.111 m: deeper gates see more uniform pressure.

If "1 m lower" means 1 m further along the slope instead, \(L_c = 2.0\) m, \(h_c = 1.0\) m, \(F_R = 20\) kN and \(L_p = 2.17\) m. Check which your instructor intends.

s29–33Q4 · Same as Q3 with 1 MPa on the surfacePressurized tank (cases #5, #6)2.03 MN, almost at the centroid
  1. Replace the surface pressure by an equivalent depth: \(h_a = p/\gamma = 10^6/10^4 = 100\) m of water.
  2. \(h_c = 100 + 1.5 = 101.5\) m, so \(F_R = (10{,}000)(101.5)(2) = 2.03\times10^6\) N. Of that, \(pA = 2.0\) MN is the surface pressure and 30 kN is the water.
  3. Measured from the equivalent surface, \(L_c = 101.5/\cos60° = 203\) m and \(L_p - L_c = \dfrac{0.667}{(203)(2)} = 0.0016\) m. With pressure this uniform, the center of pressure sits on the centroid: this is simple case #1, "depth is irrelevant."
s26Circular view port at 30°Orange drink sg 1.10; centroid 3.0 m deep; radius 2.4 m (the slide's "diameter" is a typo)585.8 kN
  1. \(A = \pi(2.4)^2 = 18.10\) m², \(\gamma = 1.10 \times 9.81 = 10.79\) kN/m³.
  2. \(F_R = \gamma h_c A = (10.79)(3.0)(18.10) = 585.8\) kN.
  3. The plane is 30° from vertical, so \(L_c = h_c/\cos30° = 3.0/0.866 = 3.464\) m.
  4. Circle: \(I_c = \pi D^4/64\), so \(I_c/A = D^2/16 = (4.8)^2/16 = 1.44\) m². \(L_p - L_c = 1.44/3.464 = 0.416\) m. All three values match the slide. Load it in the solver.

As the slide says, a 4.8 m port can't physically fit a 4.6 m tank. It's practice for the formulas.

4.9Mercury pushing out a stopper0.75 in stopper in the bottom of a vat, mercury 28.0 in deep6.05 lb
  1. Horizontal surface: \(p = \gamma h = (13.54)(62.4)(28.0/12) = 1971\) lb/ft² \(= 13.7\) psi.
  2. \(A = \pi(0.75)^2/4 = 0.442\) in², so \(F = pA = 13.7 \times 0.442 = 6.05\) lb, matching the book.
4.10Shower tank flapper valveWater 1.80 m deep; 75 mm opening; hinge 10 mm beyond the opening's edge; pull applied 65 mm from the hinge (slide 10)78 N on the valve; 57 N to open
  1. Pressure on the valve: \(p = \gamma h = 9.81 \times 1.80 = 17.66\) kPa.
  2. Water acts only over the opening (the rest of the flapper rests on the tank bottom): \(A = \pi(0.075)^2/4 = 4.42\times10^{-3}\) m², so \(F_w = pA = 78.0\) N, at the opening's center.
  3. That center is \(37.5 + 10 = 47.5\) mm from the hinge. Moments about the hinge: \(F(65) = 78.0(47.5)\), so \(F = 57.0\) N, ignoring the flapper's weight.

If your instructor only wants the force on the valve ("horizontal surface only"), the answer is 78 N.

Ex 4.8Quarter-cylinder tank bottomR = 1.50 m, w = 2.50 m, h₁ = 3.00 m, waterFR = 206.5 kN at 48.1°
  1. \(F_V\) = weight above: area \(= (3.00)(1.50) + \pi(1.50)^2/4 = 6.267\) m², so \(F_V = (9.81)(6.267)(2.50) = 153.7\) kN.
  2. \(F_H\): projection is 1.50 m tall, \(h_c = 3.75\) m, so \(F_H = (9.81)(1.50)(2.50)(3.75) = 138.0\) kN at \(h_p = 3.75 + 1.5^2/(12 \times 3.75) = 3.80\) m.
  3. \(F_R = \sqrt{153.7^2 + 138.0^2} = 206.5\) kN at \(\tan^{-1}(153.7/138.0) = 48.1°\).
  4. Check: \(F_V\) acts 0.718 m from the center line; \(F_H\) acts 0.80 m below the center. \(138.0 \times 0.80 = 110.4\) and \(153.7 \times 0.718 = 110.4\): the moments about the center cancel, so \(F_R\) passes through it.

Self-test

Check yourself

1. A vertical rectangular wall holds water with its top at the surface. The resultant acts

The pressure prism is a triangle; its centroid is h/3 above the base, i.e. L_p = 2h/3 below the surface.

2. A submerged gate is lowered deeper without changing its shape or tilt. Lp − Lc

L_p − L_c = I_c/(L_cA). Bigger L_c, smaller offset: the pressure across a deep gate is nearly uniform.

3. The vertical force on a curved surface with liquid above it equals

F_V is the weight of the fluid volume between the curve and the free surface. Option b is F_H.

4. A closed tank of water has air at 19.6 kPa(gage) above the water. To use the open-tank formulas, add to every depth

h_a = p/γ = 19.6/9.81 = 2.0 m of water (piezometric head).

Why is the center of pressure always below the centroid?answer

Pressure increases with depth, so the lower part of the surface is pushed harder than the upper part. The resultant shifts toward the high-pressure end. Mathematically \(L_p - L_c = I_c/(L_cA) > 0\) because \(I_c\), \(L_c\) and \(A\) are all positive.

Why does the resultant on a circular-arc surface pass through the center of the arc?answer

Each small pressure force is perpendicular to the surface, which on a circle means along a radius. Every one of them passes through the center, so they have zero moment about it, and so does their resultant.
