Forces from Moving Fluids

To turn a stream, something has to push on it. Newton's second law for a steady flow says how hard: the net force on the fluid equals the mass flow rate times the change in velocity, \(\vec F = \dot m(\vec v_{out} - \vec v_{in})\). The vane, the pipe bend or the support feels the equal and opposite push.

Slides 2–3, 6, 9 · Mott §16.2–16.4

Newton's second law for a flowing stream

  1. 1

    For a solid body

    \[ F = \frac{dP}{dt} = m\frac{dV}{dt} \]

    The body's momentum P changes because its velocity changes.

  2. 2

    For steady flow through a tapered pipe (slide 3)

    Nothing inside the pipe changes with time, yet the fluid speeds up. In time dt, a slug of mass \(\rho A_{out}V_{out}dt\) leaves with velocity \(V_{out}\) while \(\rho A_{in}V_{in}dt\) arrives with \(V_{in}\). The momentum in the pipe changed only by that exchange:

    \[ F = \frac{\rho A V_{out}dt\,V_{out} - \rho A V_{in}dt\,V_{in}}{dt} = \dot m\,(V_{out} - V_{in}) \]

    Continuity makes \(\rho AV\) the same at both ends: that's \(\dot m\). This bookkeeping between a fixed region and the fluid passing through it is the Reynolds transport theorem (the slide's title).

  3. 3

    In 2-D or 3-D, one equation per direction

    \[ F_x = \dot m(v_{2x} - v_{1x}),\quad F_y = \dot m(v_{2y} - v_{1y}) \]

    F is the net external force on the fluid in the control volume: from walls and supports, from pressure on the inlet and outlet faces, and gravity if it matters.

Mott's procedure (§16.3)

  1. Draw a control volume around the fluid that turns. Pick x and y.
  2. Mark the inlet and outlet velocities, with signs, and the pressure forces \(pA\) pushing inward on the inlet and outlet faces.
  3. Write \(\Sigma F = \dot m(v_{out} - v_{in})\) in each direction and solve for the unknown wall or support force.
  4. Newton's third law (slide 6): the force of the fluid on the vane is equal and opposite.
Trap

Decide up front whether you want the force on the water or on the vane: they differ only in sign, and slides 4 and 5 ask one each. A free jet in air is at atmospheric pressure, so it carries no pressure force (gauge 0).

Trap

Use gauge pressures when the outside of the pipe is at atmospheric pressure: the atmosphere pushes on the outside of the bend too, and gauge pressure accounts for that (slide 11 gives an absolute pressure; subtract atmospheric first).

Moving and accelerating bodies

A vane moving away at constant speed V0 (slide 19): ride along with it. Relative to the vane the jet arrives at \(V - V_0\), and only \(\rho A(V - V_0)\) of mass reaches it each second (the jet has to catch up). With a 12 m/s relative speed, a 20 m/s jet on a car doing 8 m/s feels exactly like a 12 m/s jet on a fixed vane: same force.

A wheel of vanes (a Pelton turbine, slide 24): a new vane is always in the jet, so all the jet's mass flow \(\rho AV\) does work. Power = force × blade speed.

Terminal speed with drag (slide 20): the jet pushes with \(c(U - V)^2\), where \(c = \rho A(1 - \cos\theta)\), and air drag pulls back \(kV^2\). They balance when \(\sqrt c\,(U - V) = \sqrt k\,V\).

Accelerating from rest (slides 22–23): \(m\,dV/dt = c(U - V)^2\) separates and integrates to

\[ \frac{V}{U} = \frac{(cU/m)\,t}{1 + (cU/m)\,t} \]
Slide 23

The slide writes the left side as U/V; it must be V/U (V starts at 0 and approaches U). Also, \(\rho A(1 + \cos45°) = 1000(1.963\times10^{-3})(1.707) = 3.35\), which the slide rounds to 3.5, so \(cU/m\) is 0.67 rather than 0.7.

Rockets (slide 21): the exhaust leaves at \(v_e\) relative to the rocket, so thrust \(= \dot m v_e\).

Slides 14–16

The Coanda effect

A stream tends to cling to a curved surface next to it: tea runs down the outside of a tipped cup, and a faucet stream bends around the back of a spoon. Viscosity drags the thin layer of air between the jet and the surface along until it's thinned out, the pressure there drops, and the jet is pulled onto the surface. Once it follows the curve, momentum takes over: turning the stream toward one side means the surface pushes the water that way, so the water pulls the surface the other way, into the stream. A ladle held under a faucet is drawn toward the water, not pushed away (slide 15).

Aircraft use it (slide 16): slats and flaps help the airflow follow the wing's curved upper surface at high angles of attack, turning more air downward and making more lift. This is the other half of the Magnus effect story from Chapter 6.

s4–5Ex 16.1 · jet turned 90° by a vane1 in water jet at 20 ft/s, turned straight upon water: −4.23i + 4.23j lb
  1. \(A = \pi(1/12)^2/4 = 5.45\times10^{-3}\) ft², \(\dot m = \rho AV = 1.94(5.45\times10^{-3})(20) = 0.212\) slug/s.
  2. x: \(F_x = \dot m(0 - 20) = -4.23\) lb. y: \(F_y = \dot m(20 - 0) = +4.23\) lb. That's the force the plate applies on the water (slide 4).
  3. Slide 5's variation, the force of the water on the plate: \(+4.23\) lb in x and \(-4.23\) lb in y (Newton's third law).
s7LevitationAn 825 N plate held up by a 60 mm water jet that spreads out sideways17.1 m/s
  1. The jet arrives going up and leaves horizontally in all directions, so its vertical momentum is destroyed: \(F = \dot mV = \rho AV^2\).
  2. \(1000\,\pi(0.03)^2 V^2 = 825\) gives \(V = 17.1\) m/s at the plate. Same as Ex 16.1 with a different geometry, as the slide says.
s8Ex 16.2 · 90° elbow4 in Sch 40, 3000 L/min of water, 550 kPa in, 440 kPa outR = −4820i + 3920j N
  1. \(A = 8.21\times10^{-3}\) m², \(Q = 0.05\) m³/s, \(v = 6.09\) m/s, \(\dot m = 50\) kg/s, \(\dot mv = 304\) N.
  2. Pressure forces: \(p_1A_1 = 4517\) N pushing in +x on the inlet face; \(p_2A_2 = 3614\) N pushing in −y on the outlet face.
  3. x: \(R_x + 4517 = 50(0 - 6.09)\), so \(R_x = -4821\) N. y: \(R_y - 3614 = 50(6.09 - 0)\), so \(R_y = 3918\) N. The slide rounds to −4800i + 3900j N. Load it in the bend calculator.
s102023 Final · jet split by a cone2 cm jet at 20 m/s split symmetrically, leaving at θ to the axis; speed unchanged−36.8i N (45°); −125.7i N (90°)
  1. \(\dot m = 1000\,\pi(0.01)^2(20) = 6.28\) kg/s, \(\dot mV = 125.7\) N. The two halves' y-momenta cancel.
  2. x on the water: \(F_x = \dot m(V\cos\theta - V) = -125.7(1 - \cos\theta)\). The water pushes the cone with the opposite force, so the hand must supply \(F = -125.7(1 - \cos\theta)\,\hat i\): −36.8 N at 45° and −125.7 N at 90°.
s11Reducing elbow discharging to airInlet 220 kPa absolute, 0.01 m²; outlet 0.0025 m² at 16 m/s pointing down, to atmosphereR = −1350i − 640j N
  1. \(v_1 = 16(0.0025/0.01) = 4\) m/s, \(\dot m = 1000(0.0025)(16) = 40\) kg/s.
  2. Gauge inlet pressure: 220 − 101 = 119 kPa (the atmosphere also acts on the outside). Outlet: 0 gauge.
  3. x: \(R_x + (119\,000)(0.01) = 40(0 - 4)\), \(R_x = -1350\) N. y: \(R_y = 40(-16 - 0) = -640\) N. Weight neglected.
s12Garden hose with a thumbHose area 4 × 10⁻⁴ m², 2 m/s, 0 gauge at the open end; block 60 % of the outlet−1.8 N on the water
  1. Assuming the same flow (Ch 6 hose problem): the outlet is 40 % open, so the jet leaves at 5 m/s. \(\dot m = 1000(4\times10^{-4})(2) = 0.8\) kg/s.
  2. Bernoulli gives the pressure just upstream of the thumb: \(p_1 = \tfrac{1000}{2}(5^2 - 2^2) = 10.5\) kPa, so \(p_1A_1 = 4.2\) N pushing downstream.
  3. Momentum: \(4.2 + F = 0.8(5 - 2) = 2.4\), so \(F = -1.8\) N: the thumb pushes back on the water with 1.8 N, and the water pushes the thumb forward with 1.8 N.

"Doesn't make sense" at first: the water speeds up, so why isn't the thumb pulled back? Because the pressure that builds up behind the thumb is bigger than the momentum change.

s13Sluice gate3 m deep at 1 m/s upstream; 0.429 m at 7 m/s downstream; hydrostatic pressure at both sections−25.2 kN per metre of width
  1. Per metre of width: \(\dot m = 1000(3)(1) = 3000\) kg/s.
  2. Pressure forces \(\tfrac12\rho gD^2\): 44.1 kN/m upstream (pushing +x), 0.90 kN/m downstream (pushing −x).
  3. \(R_x + 44.1 - 0.90 = 3000(7 - 1)/1000 = 18.0\) kN/m, so \(R_x = -25.2\) kN/m: the gate holds the water back with 25.2 kN per metre. With the gate closed and still water it would be the full 44.1 kN/m; flow relieves it.
s15Coanda · ladle under a faucet1 m/s, 1 cm² stream, turned 30° by the ladle; gravity ignored−0.05 N

\(\dot m = 1000(10^{-4})(1) = 0.1\) kg/s. The stream arrives vertically and leaves with a horizontal component \(1 \times \sin30° = 0.5\) m/s, so the ladle pushes the water with \(0.1 \times 0.5 = 0.05\) N horizontally. The water pulls the ladle 0.05 N the other way, into the stream.

s17Conveyor belt filling with sandBelt at 3 ft/s; sand falls at 500 lbm/s46.6 lbf

The sand arrives with no horizontal velocity and leaves at the belt speed. \(T = \dot mV_{belt} = (500/32.2)(3) = 46.6\) lbf. The same momentum idea works for any stream of mass, not just fluids.

s18–19Vane turning a jet 135°, fixed and moving5 cm water jet at 12 m/s; then 20 m/s on a car moving at 8 m/s483i − 200j N both times
  1. Fixed: \(\dot m = 1000\,\pi(0.025)^2(12) = 23.6\) kg/s, \(\dot mV = 283\) N. The jet leaves at 135° from its arrival direction: on the water, \(F_x = 283(\cos135° - 1) = -483\) N, \(F_y = 283\sin135° = 200\) N. On the vane: \(483\hat i - 200\hat j\) N.
  2. Moving at 8 m/s: relative speed 12 m/s and relative mass flow \(\rho A(12)\): identical numbers. Power to the car: 483 × 8 = 3.86 kW.
s20–23Car pushed by the jet: drag, acceleration, speed over time20 m/s jet, 5 cm, 135° vane; 100 kg; drag 10V²7.3 m/s; 4.83 m/s²; V/U = 0.67t/(1 + 0.67t)
  1. \(c = \rho A(1 - \cos135°) = 3.35\) kg/m. Terminal speed: \(3.35(20 - V)^2 = 10V^2\), so \(1.831(20 - V) = 3.162V\), \(V = 7.33\) m/s.
  2. At 8 m/s with no drag: \(a = 3.35(12)^2/100 = 4.83\) m/s².
  3. From rest with no drag: \(\int dV/(U - V)^2 = (c/m)\int dt\) gives \(V/U = 0.670t/(1 + 0.670t)\) (t in seconds). After 1 s the car does 8.0 m/s. See the plot in the moving-bodies section.
s21Rocket thrust15.2 lbm/s of propellant, exhaust at 3220 ft/s relative1520 lbf

\(F = \dot mv_e = (15.2/32.2)(3220) = 1520\) lbf. Constant velocity means the thrust balances drag and the rocket's weight component; in the rocket's frame the propellant arrives at rest and leaves at 3220 ft/s.

s24Turbine blade (Mott 16.29–16.30)7.5 mm jet at 25 m/s, arriving 10° above the blade's direction of motion and leaving along the blade at 60° from the reverse direction; blades at 10 m/s≈ 245 W (wheel)
  1. Check the geometry with Problem 16.29 (blade fixed): \(\dot mV = 1000(4.42\times10^{-5})(25)^2 = 27.6\) N; \(F_x = 27.6(\cos60° + \cos10°) = 41.0\) N and \(F_y = 27.6(\sin60° - \sin10°) = 19.1\) N, the book's answers.
  2. Blade moving at 10 m/s: relative inlet velocity \((24.62 - 10,\ 4.34) = (14.62, 4.34)\), 15.25 m/s. It leaves along the blade at the same relative speed: \((-7.63, 13.21)\), so the absolute exit velocity is \((2.37, 13.21)\) m/s.
  3. As part of a wheel, all \(\dot m = \rho AV = 1.10\) kg/s does work: \(F_x = 1.10(24.62 - 2.37) = 24.6\) N on the blades, power \(= 24.6 \times 10 = 245\) W, 71 % of the jet's 345 W of kinetic power.

The blade angles are read from the slide's figure, and Mott's answer to 16.30 isn't in the back of the book, so treat this as my worked solution. A single blade alone (not a wheel) receives only \(\rho A|w| = 0.674\) kg/s and gets 150 W.

Self-test

Check yourself

1. A jet is turned straight back (180°) by a fixed vane, speed unchanged. Compared with a flat plate (90°, splashing sideways), the force is

F = ṁV(1 − cos θ): 1 at 90°, 2 at 180°. Pelton buckets turn the jet back for this reason.

2. A vane moves away from a jet at half the jet speed. Compared with a fixed vane, the force is

Relative speed halves and the mass flow reaching it halves: (1/2)² = 1/4.

3. In a bend calculation, which pressure belongs in pA?

The atmosphere pushes on the outside of the pipe too; gauge pressure nets that out (slide 11).
