Noncircular Sections

Air ducts are rectangular and heat exchangers push fluid through the ring between two tubes. Chapter 8's Darcy equation still works if you replace the diameter with four times the hydraulic radius, \(R = A/WP\). The deck opens with a detour worth taking: four different "lines" people draw to see a flow.

Slides 2–3 ("off topic, but important")

Pathline, streakline, timeline, streamline

  • Pathline (blue dot and trail): the trajectory of one particle over time. A long-exposure photo of one glowing particle.
  • Streakline (pink dye): every particle that has passed through one fixed point, seen at one instant. Smoke from a chimney, dye from a needle.
  • Timeline (amber): a line of particles marked at the same moment, then watched as it moves. In a pipe it bends into the shape of the velocity profile (slide 3).
  • Streamline (gray dashes): a curve tangent to the velocity everywhere at one instant. You can't photograph it directly; it's a mathematical picture of the velocity field.

In steady flow the velocity at each point never changes, so a particle follows the streamline it starts on: pathlines, streaklines and streamlines are the same curves (slide 3). Switch the demo to "Flapping flow" and they come apart.

What the demo is computing

Flapping flow: a uniform stream \(u = U\) with a sideways velocity that swings in time, \(v = V\sin\omega t\). At any instant every streamline is a straight line at angle \(\tan^{-1}(v/u)\), but a particle's path snakes, and the dye released from the needle traces yet another curve.

Steady flow: flow between two plates with the laminar profile \(u = u_{max}[1 - (y/h)^2]\). The timeline, released straight across, stretches into a parabola: the profile you'll see in the next section.

Slides 4–6 · Mott §9.2–9.4 · review of Chapter 8

Velocity profiles: laminar vs turbulent

The velocity in a pipe is zero at the wall and largest on the centerline. How it gets there is the difference between the two regimes.

Laminar (slide 5): a force balance on a cylinder of fluid of radius r, \(-\Delta p\,\pi r^2 = \eta\,\frac{dv}{dr}\,2\pi r L\), integrates to a parabola:

\[ v(r) = \frac{\Delta p}{4\eta L}\left(R^2 - r^2\right) = 2\bar v\left[1 - \left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^2\right] \]

The average velocity is half the maximum, and the head loss is the Hagen–Poiseuille equation:

\[ h_L = \frac{32\eta L\bar v}{\gamma D^2} = \frac{128\,\eta L Q}{\pi\gamma D^4} \]
Slide 5

The slide writes the last form as \(128\eta LQ/(\gamma D^4)\), dropping the π. Substituting \(\bar v = 4Q/\pi D^2\) shows the π belongs in the denominator.

Turbulent (slide 6): eddies mix fast fluid toward the wall, so the profile is much flatter, with a steep drop right at the wall. Mott's Eq. 9–7 approximates it:

\[ U = \bar v\left[1 + 1.43\sqrt f + 2.15\sqrt f\,\log_{10}(y/r_o)\right] \]

with y measured from the wall. On the centerline \(U_{max} = \bar v(1 + 1.43\sqrt f)\), only about 20 % above the average.

Slides 7–11 · Mott §9.5

The hydraulic radius

Friction acts where fluid touches a wall; the fluid's momentum is carried by its cross-section. The ratio of the two is the hydraulic radius.

\[ R = \frac{A}{WP} = \frac{\text{flow area}}{\text{wetted perimeter}} \]

Wetted perimeter is every boundary the fluid touches: for the ring between two tubes, both the outside of the inner tube and the inside of the outer one.

Slide 10 answers.

  • Circle: \(R = \dfrac{\pi D^2/4}{\pi D} = \dfrac{D}{4}\). That's why \(D = 4R\): the substitution gives back the circular pipe exactly.
  • Square of side a: \(R = \dfrac{a^2}{4a} = \dfrac{a}{4}\), so \(D_h = a\).
  • Rectangle a × b: \(R = \dfrac{ab}{2(a+b)}\). For \(a \gg b\), \(R \to b/2\) and \(D_h \to 2b\): a wide, thin slot behaves like a pipe twice the gap.

Then use \(4R\) wherever the diameter appeared (slide 11):

\[ N_R = \frac{\rho\,\bar v\,(4R)}{\eta},\qquad h_L = f\,\frac{L}{4R}\,\frac{\bar v^2}{2g},\qquad \frac{\varepsilon}{D}\to\frac{\varepsilon}{4R} \]

Mott's caution: it works well in turbulent flow for shapes that aren't too elongated. Laminar flow in noncircular ducts has its own exact solutions (slide 8 skips them because they're rare).

s12Practice #1 · same velocity in shell and tube (Mott 9.19)Outer tube 7/8 in OD, inner tube ½ in OD, both 0.049 in wallQshell/Qtube = 2.19
  1. Tube inside diameter: 0.500 − 2(0.049) = 0.402 in. Shell: inside of the outer tube 0.875 − 0.098 = 0.777 in, minus the outside of the inner tube, 0.500 in.
  2. Same velocity means \(Q \propto A\): \(\dfrac{Q_{shell}}{Q_{tube}} = \dfrac{0.777^2 - 0.500^2}{0.402^2} = \dfrac{0.3537}{0.1616} = 2.19\). Matches the book.
s13Practice #2 · Reynolds numbers in the heat exchangerTube: 4.75 gal/min water at 200 °F. Shell: 30.0 gal/min ethylene glycol at 77 °Ftube ≈ 1.2 × 10⁵; shell ≈ 5.0–5.5 × 10³
  1. Tube (an ordinary pipe, as in Ch 8): \(A = \pi(0.402/12)^2/4 = 8.81\times10^{-4}\) ft², \(Q = 4.75/449 = 0.01058\) ft³/s, \(\bar v = 12.0\) ft/s. Water at 200 °F: \(\nu = 3.34\times10^{-6}\) ft²/s (Mott Table A.2), so \(N_R = (12.0)(0.0335)/3.34\times10^{-6} = 1.20\times10^5\).
  2. Shell: \(A = \pi(0.777^2 - 0.500^2)/4 = 0.278\) in²; \(WP = \pi(0.777 + 0.500) = 4.01\) in; \(R = 0.06925\) in, as on the slide. In fact \(R = (D_o - d_i)/4\) for any annulus.
  3. \(\bar v = (30.0/449)/(0.278/144) = 34.6\) ft/s, \(4R = 0.2770\) in = 0.02308 ft.
  4. With the slide's viscosity (15 cP = 3.13 × 10⁻⁴ lb·s/ft²) and ρ = 1.1(1.94) = 2.13 slug/ft³: \(N_R = (2.13)(34.6)(0.02308)/3.13\times10^{-4} = 5450\). With Mott Table B.2's value for ethylene glycol at 77 °F (3.38 × 10⁻⁴), \(N_R = 5050\). Turbulent either way, barely.

This is Problem 9.26 in the 8th edition; the slide shows it with an older edition's number, 9.25. The book doesn't list the answer to even-numbered problems, so these are my own numbers.

s14Practice #3 · head loss in the shell100 ft of shell, ε = 0.01 in≈ 5400 ft
  1. \(\varepsilon/4R = 0.01/0.277 = 0.036\): very rough relative to such a narrow gap.
  2. Colebrook at \(N_R \approx 5450\): \(f = 0.067\).
  3. \(h_L = f\dfrac{L}{4R}\dfrac{\bar v^2}{2g} = 0.067 \times \dfrac{100}{0.02308} \times \dfrac{34.6^2}{64.4} = 5370\) ft, the slide's "~5500 ft". Load it in the duct solver.

The slide gives 34.2 ft/s; Q/A works out to 34.6 ft/s, a 1 % difference that doesn't change the conclusion. Sanity check: 5400 ft of glycol is a pressure drop of about 2600 psi over 100 ft. Pushing 30 gal/min through a 0.28 in² gap is an extreme design, and a real exchanger would use a much bigger shell. With drawn-tubing roughness (5 × 10⁻⁶ ft) instead of 0.01 in, the loss drops to about 3000 ft.

Textbook problems, solved

Ex 9.5Hydraulic radius of a square duct around a tubeSquare 250 mm inside, tube 150 mm ODR = 30.5 mm
  1. \(A = 250^2 - \pi(150)^2/4 = 44{,}829\) mm².
  2. \(WP = 4(250) + \pi(150) = 1471\) mm (both the duct's inside and the tube's outside are wetted).
  3. \(R = 44{,}829/1471 = 30.5\) mm.
Ex 9.6Reynolds number in that ductEthylene glycol at 25 °C (ρ = 1100 kg/m³, η = 1.62 × 10⁻² Pa·s), 0.16 m³/sNR = 2.96 × 10⁴
  1. \(\bar v = 0.16/0.0448 = 3.57\) m/s.
  2. \(N_R = \dfrac{\rho\bar v(4R)}{\eta} = \dfrac{(1100)(3.57)(0.122)}{1.62\times10^{-2}} = 2.96\times10^4\). Turbulent.
Ex 9.7Pressure drop over 50 mε = 3 × 10⁻⁵ m, horizontal ducthL = 6.52 m; Δp = 70.4 kPa
  1. \(4R/\varepsilon = 0.122/3\times10^{-5} = 4067\); the Moody diagram gives \(f = 0.0245\).
  2. \(h_L = 0.0245 \times \dfrac{50}{0.122} \times \dfrac{3.57^2}{2(9.81)} = 6.52\) m.
  3. Horizontal, same velocity: \(\Delta p = \gamma h_L = 10.79 \times 6.52 = 70.4\) kPa. Load it in the duct solver.

The duct solver uses the Colebrook equation, which gives f = 0.0242, hL = 6.45 m and Δp = 69.6 kPa. The 1 % difference is how precisely anyone can read a Moody chart.

Self-test

Check yourself

1. In steady flow, a streakline from a dye needle

Steady flow: pathlines, streaklines and streamlines coincide. The timeline is the one that shows the velocity profile.

2. The hydraulic radius of a circular pipe of diameter D is

πD²/4 ÷ πD = D/4. It's not the geometric radius, which is why 4R (not 2R) replaces D.

3. Laminar flow in a pipe: the centerline velocity is

Parabolic profile: v_max = 2v̄. In turbulent flow it's closer to 1.2v̄.

4. For the ring between two tubes, the wetted perimeter is

The fluid touches both walls. That makes R = (D_o − d_i)/4.
