Part I · Slides 4–6 · Mott §5.2

Archimedes' principle

Where does the buoyant force come from? From Chapter 3: pressure grows with depth, so the bottom of a body is pushed up harder than its top is pushed down.

  1. 1

    Pressure on top and bottom

    Take a block of horizontal area \(A\) and height \(H\), its top at depth \(h\). The fluid pushes down on the top with \(\gamma_f h A\) and up on the bottom with \(\gamma_f (h+H) A\). The side pressures cancel in pairs.

  2. 2

    Subtract

    \[ F_b = \gamma_f (h + H)A - \gamma_f h A = \gamma_f (HA) = \gamma_f V \]

    The depth \(h\) cancels. A fully submerged body feels the same buoyant force at any depth (for an incompressible fluid).

  3. 3

    Any shape

    Replace the body by fluid of the same shape: that fluid would sit still, so the surrounding pressure must hold up exactly its weight. The pressure doesn't know what's inside the boundary, so

    \[ F_b = \gamma_f V_d \]

    \(V_d\) is the displaced volume: the part of the body below the surface. \(F_b\) acts straight up through the centroid of \(V_d\), the center of buoyancy.

The four situations on slide 5

  • Completely submerged and held: an external force makes up the difference between weight and buoyancy.
  • Floating: the body sinks until \(\gamma_f V_d = W\). For a uniform body, the submerged fraction is \(\text{sg}_{body}/\text{sg}_{fluid}\).
  • Tending to float: average specific weight below the fluid's. Held under, it needs a downward force.
  • Tending to sink: average specific weight above the fluid's. It needs an upward force (or the bottom supplies it).

Two bodies on a tether

With two bodies, draw two free-body diagrams. The tether tension appears in both, pulling the bodies toward each other, and links the two equations.

Slide 13. A buoy of volume 1 m³ (sg ≈ 0) floats half out of the water. It is tethered to a 2 m³ weight hanging below it. Find the weight's density and the tether tension.

  1. Buoy: it has no weight, so \(T = F_{b,buoy} = \gamma_w (0.5\ \text{m}^3) = 9810 \times 0.5 = 4905\) N.
  2. Weight: \(W = F_{b,wt} + T\), so \(\rho g (2) = 9810(2) + 4905\), which gives \(\rho = 1250\) kg/m³ (sg 1.25).

Use the tool to see what happens when the weight is denser: the buoy sinks lower until, at \(\rho = 1500\) kg/m³, it is completely under and the pair sinks.

Part II · Slides 18–19 · Mott §5.4

Stability of a submerged body

A body is stable if, after a small tilt, the forces on it push it back. On a completely submerged body, the weight acts down through the center of gravity (cg) and the buoyant force acts up through the center of buoyancy (cb). The cb is the centroid of the body's outside shape, so it doesn't move relative to the body when the body tilts.

  • cg below cb: a tilt offsets the two forces into a couple that rotates the body back. Stable. Submarines and research submersibles put batteries and ballast low and foam high (slide 19).
  • cg above cb: the couple rotates it further. Unstable.
  • cg at cb (a solid, uniform body): no couple at any angle. Neutral.

Slides 20–24 · Mott §5.5–5.6

Stability of a floating body: the metacenter

Most ships carry their cg above their cb, and they don't capsize. The difference from the submarine: when a floating body tilts, its displaced volume changes shape, and the cb moves toward the side that went down.

  • Metacenter (mc): where the vertical line through the new cb crosses the body's centerline, for a small tilt (slide 21). For small angles it stays at one point.
  • Test: the floating body is stable if its cg is below the metacenter. Then the weight and the buoyant force form a righting couple.
  • Where is it? A distance \(MB = I/V_d\) above the cb. \(I\) is the smallest second moment of the waterplane area (the body's cross section at the water surface), about its centroidal axis; \(V_d\) is the displaced volume.
  • Metacentric height \(MG = y_{mc} - y_{cg}\) measures how stable. Mott cites at least 1.5 ft (0.46 m) for small seagoing vessels and more than 3.5 ft (1.07 m) for large ships. Too large makes a stiff, fast roll that causes seasickness (slide 25).

Mott's 9-step stability check

1. Draft X = W/(γfA)
2. ycb = X/2
4–6. MB = I/Vd
7. ymc = ycb + MB
8–9. Metacentric height MG
Trap

Use the smallest \(I\): a boat tips about its long axis. For a box hull that is \(I = LB^3/12\) with B the beam, not \(BL^3/12\).

Trap

\(V_d\) is the submerged volume, not the hull's total volume. More load means more \(V_d\), smaller MB, and a higher cg: Problem 5.47 shows a loaded scow needs a wider hull.

Trap

Measure \(y_{cb}\), \(y_{cg}\) and \(y_{mc}\) from the same reference, usually the bottom of the hull.

Slide 25

How much does an off-center load tilt the boat?

A ship of weight W and metacentric height GM takes on a small weight w a distance Y off the centerline (W ≫ w). The combined cg moves sideways by \(wY/(W+w)\). The ship heels until the new cg is directly under the metacenter, which, for a small angle, sits GM above the cg on the old centerline:

\[ \tan\theta = \frac{wY/(W+w)}{GM} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad GM\,(W+w)\tan\theta = wY \]

That is the slide's formula. Turn it around and it is how ships measure GM: an inclining experiment moves a known weight across the deck and measures the heel.

Heel angle θ

Default: an 80 kg person (0.8 kN) stepping 1 m off center on Mott's Example 5.5 flatboat (GM = 0.18 m from Example 5.7).

s8Exercise #1 · Styrofoam vs copperSame-size cylinders held at the same depthSame buoyancy

\(F_b = \gamma_f V_d\) depends only on the fluid and the displaced volume. Same volume under water, same buoyant force. What differs is the force you apply: you push the foam down and hold the copper up, which the slide says this question is not about.

s10Exercise #2 · Pulling out a copper block9 kg in air, sg ≈ 978.5 N under water; 88.3 N in air
  1. Volume: \(V = m/\rho = 9/9000 = 0.001\) m³. Weight \(W = mg = 88.3\) N.
  2. Buoyant force while submerged: \(F_b = 9810 \times 0.001 = 9.81\) N.
  3. Force to lift it while it's under water: \(F = W - F_b = 78.5\) N. As it breaks the surface, \(V_d\) drops and the force climbs to the full 88.3 N once it's out.

Shortcut: sg 9 means the water takes away 1/9 of its weight.

s12Exercise #3 · When does it float?And what fraction floats at sg 0.9?sg < 1; 10 % above water
  1. A submerged body rises only if \(F_b > W\), i.e. \(\gamma_f V > \gamma_b V\): its specific gravity must be less than 1 (less than the fluid's, in general).
  2. Floating: \(\gamma_w V_d = \gamma_b V\), so \(V_d/V = \text{sg} = 0.9\). 90 % is under, 10 % floats above. Ice (sg about 0.92) in seawater (1.03) is Mott Problem 5.20.

Worked in the two-body section. Free body of the buoy gives T; free body of the weight gives its density.

s15Plate with a passenger2 m × 2 m plate, 30 cm thick, sg 0.3; 80 kg person steps on9 cm, then 11 cm
  1. Alone: submerged fraction = sg = 0.3, so draft = 0.3 × 30 cm = 9 cm.
  2. The person adds 80 kg, which must be carried by extra displaced water: \(\Delta V = 80/1000 = 0.08\) m³ spread over 4 m², so \(\Delta x = 0.02\) m.
  3. New draft = 11 cm, still less than the 30 cm thickness, so the plate keeps floating. It would take \(4 \times (0.30 - 0.09) \times 1000 = 840\) kg to sink it to the deck.
s24Location of the metacenter (Mott Ex 5.5)Flatboat 2.4 m × 6.0 m, 150 kN, cg 0.80 m upymc = 0.98 m > 0.80 m: stable
  1. Draft: \(X = W/(\gamma_f BL) = 150/(9.81 \times 2.4 \times 6.0) = 1.06\) m; \(y_{cb} = 0.53\) m.
  2. \(V_d = 6.0 \times 2.4 \times 1.06 = 15.3\) m³; \(I = LB^3/12 = 6.0(2.4)^3/12 = 6.91\) m⁴.
  3. \(MB = 6.91/15.3 = 0.45\) m, so \(y_{mc} = 0.98\) m. That is above \(y_{cg} = 0.80\) m: stable, with \(MG = 0.18\) m (Mott Ex 5.7). Load it in the stability check.

Textbook problems, solved

5.46River scow, minimum widthBox hull 24 ft tall, cg at mid-height (12 ft), floats 8.00 ft deep in seawater27.7 ft
  1. \(y_{cb} = 8/2 = 4\) ft. For a box, \(MB = \dfrac{LB^3/12}{LB\,X} = \dfrac{B^2}{12X} = \dfrac{B^2}{96}\). The length cancels, and so does seawater's specific weight (the draft is given).
  2. Neutral stability: \(y_{cb} + MB = y_{cg}\), so \(4 + B^2/96 = 12\), \(B^2 = 768\), \(B_{min} = 27.7\) ft.

The 24 ft height is read from the slide's figure (cg at the centroid, 12 ft up). Rounding up, any width above 27.7 ft is stable.

5.47Same scow loaded with coalDraft 16.0 ft, cg 13.5 ft above the bottom32.5 ft
  1. \(y_{cb} = 8\) ft, \(MB = B^2/(12 \times 16) = B^2/192\).
  2. \(8 + B^2/192 = 13.5\), \(B^2 = 1056\), \(B_{min} = 32.5\) ft. Matches the book. Loading a hull deeper both raises its cg and shrinks MB.
Ex 5.6Upright cylinder in oilD = 3.0 ft, H = 6.0 ft, 1550 lb, oil sg 0.90unstable (ymc 2.09 ft < ycg 3.00 ft)
  1. Draft: \(X = \dfrac{4W}{\pi D^2\gamma_o} = \dfrac{4(1550)}{\pi(9)(0.9 \times 62.4)} = 3.90\) ft; \(y_{cb} = 1.95\) ft.
  2. \(I = \pi D^4/64 = 3.98\) ft⁴, \(V_d = 27.6\) ft³, \(MB = 0.144\) ft, \(y_{mc} = 2.09\) ft.
  3. The cg is at 3.00 ft, above the metacenter: it falls over and floats on its side. Tall, narrow floating bodies tend to be unstable upright.
Ex 5.1Bronze cube held under0.50 m cube, γ = 86.9 kN/m³, in water and in mercury9.64 kN up; 5.74 kN down
  1. \(V = 0.125\) m³, \(W = 86.9 \times 0.125 = 10.86\) kN.
  2. Water: \(F_b = 9.81 \times 0.125 = 1.23\) kN, so hold it up with \(10.86 - 1.23 = 9.64\) kN.
  3. Mercury: \(F_b = 13.54 \times 9.81 \times 0.125 = 16.60\) kN, greater than W, so hold it down with 5.74 kN. Bronze floats on mercury.
s14Sphere on a pulleyProblem shown on slide 14 (not from Mott): 0.025 m³ sphere half in water, held by a rope over two pulleys to a hanging 10 kg massγ ≈ 8.83 kN/m³; floats 90 % submerged

Assumptions (the problem asks for them): frictionless pulleys, a weightless rope, the sphere submerged exactly to its center as the figure appears to show, fresh water at 9.81 kN/m³.

  1. Rope tension = weight of the hanging mass: \(T = 10 \times 9.81 = 98.1\) N.
  2. Sphere: \(W = F_b + T = 9810(0.0125) + 98.1 = 122.6 + 98.1 = 220.7\) N.
  3. \(\gamma = W/V = 220.7/0.025 = 8830\) N/m³ (sg 0.90).
  4. Remove the mass and the sphere floats with 90 % of its volume submerged, deeper than before.

I don't have this book's answer key, so check the numbers against your instructor's solution. If the sphere in the figure is not exactly half submerged, step 2 changes.

s16Inverted bowl in a dense liquid (advanced)Problem shown on slide 16 (not from Mott): bowl of 0.9 L of clay (sg 6.1), 100 mm inside diameter, held rim-down 200 mm deep in sg 15.6 liquid; the liquid rises 20 mm inside≈ 156 N downward

Key idea: take the bowl and its trapped air as one body. The air–liquid interface is at hydrostatic pressure, so Archimedes applies to the combined volume. The air's weight is negligible.

  1. Air volume: I read the slide's 80 mm as the inside height from the rim, so the air column is \(80 - 20 = 60\) mm tall: \(V_{air} = \pi(0.05)^2(0.060) = 0.471\) L.
  2. Buoyancy: \(F_b = (15.6 \times 9810)(0.9 + 0.471)\times10^{-3} = 153{,}040 \times 1.371\times10^{-3} = 209.8\) N.
  3. Weight: \(W = (6.1 \times 9810)(0.9\times10^{-3}) = 53.9\) N.
  4. Hold-down force: \(209.8 - 53.9 = 156\) N downward.

The 200 mm depth doesn't enter the answer once the 20 mm rise is given (it would only matter for working out how much the air compresses). If the 80 mm is the outside height instead, the inside height and the answer change, and the figure alone doesn't settle that. I don't have this book's answer key.

Self-test

Check yourself

1. A steel ball rests on the bottom of a pool. You move it to a deeper spot. Its buoyant force

F_b = γ_f V_d. Depth cancels: both top and bottom pressures rise by the same amount.

2. A log floats with 70 % of its volume submerged in fresh water. Its specific gravity is

γ_w V_d = γ_log V, so sg = V_d/V = 0.70.

3. A floating box hull has its cg above its center of buoyancy. It is

cg below cb is the test for submerged bodies. Floating bodies use the metacenter, which can be well above the cg (Ex 5.5).

4. Doubling a box hull's beam (same length, same weight) multiplies MB by

I = LB³/12 grows 8×. V_d stays the same (same weight), so MB = I/V_d grows 8×. Beam is the cheapest way to buy stability.

Why does a floating body's center of buoyancy move when it tilts, but a submerged body's doesn't?answer

The cb is the centroid of the displaced volume. A submerged body always displaces its whole outside shape, which tilts with it, so the cb stays fixed relative to the body. A floating body displaces only the part below the waterline; tilting pushes a wedge of hull under on one side and lifts one out on the other, so the displaced shape, and its centroid, shifts toward the low side.
